Comparison of the two analyses of California Proposition 40: Open Output (here) · Open Analysis: Report · Open Materials · Overview

Why do the two analyses of Proposition 40 disagree?

The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, August 2026) finds that the one-time billionaire tax raises money for California, net of the income tax it changes. The RJKDC analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026) finds that it loses money, because it counts the income tax lost to billionaires who leave for ever. This page puts both on one definition, the net fiscal effect, and lines up the assumptions the two analyses disagree on. Each switch below moves one assumption from the BSZ value to the RJKDC value. With every switch off you get the BSZ analysis's estimate; with every switch on you get the RJKDC analysis's. Every number is precomputed by the pipeline that reproduces both.

The RJKDC values in the switches above are those of the RJKDC analysis's March 2026 version (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso, SSRN), the last version whose inputs are published. The September 2026 revision (Jaros and Rauh, NBER) changes things the common function cannot take as inputs: it weights the revenue by the chance that the tax survives litigation, grows the base 7% to the end of 2026, and draws the share of billionaires who leave independently of the revenue. This switch adds that revision step (−$14.3B here) plus the model difference (+$0.10B), the small gap between the common function at the March 2026 inputs and that version's own Monte Carlo, which uses rounded numbers.

Mean NPV, the exact expectation of the authors' Monte Carlo

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax (Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the present value of the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted for 5 years and discounted at 3%. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The RJKDC analysis's is a perpetuity net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER c15504, September 2026).

The net fiscal effect puts both on one definition, and the two cards are its two ends. The BSZ end is the same $106.8B, with the 5 years discounted over the RJKDC analysis's 1.5% to 4.5% rate range instead of at 3%. The RJKDC end is −$39.0B, the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, of which the authors' seeded run gives −$38.9B.

The bridge: how much each switch moves the estimate

The switchboard shows one mix of switches at a time. The bridge below adds up the whole move, from the BSZ end to the RJKDC end, as a waterfall chart. The BSZ end is $106.8B and the RJKDC end −$39.0B, a gap of $145.8B. Each bar between the two ends is the effect of one switch. The last two bars belong to the revision switch: the model difference and the September 2026 revision. Together the bars add up to −$145.8B. The bars of switches that are off above are faded.

One estimating function, score_common() (comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin), computes both analyses. Each disputed input is one of its arguments and takes either the BSZ value or the RJKDC value; a switch flips one argument. With every input at the BSZ value the function returns the BSZ end exactly. With every input at the RJKDC value it returns the Monte Carlo expectation of the RJKDC version those values come from (the revision switch's note says which), up to a $0.10B model difference. The bridge runs in the direction of Figure 2 of Hoopes (2026), the existing comparison described on the overview.

What a switch adds depends on which switches are already on, because the inputs interact: a departure matters more when the base is larger. So the bars can be sized two ways. The default bars are Shapley values: each switch's effect averaged over all 39,916,800 orders in which the switches could be flipped, so the bars add up exactly to the gap whatever the reading order. "One order" shows a single sequence instead, the one Hoopes reads in. This setting changes the bridge only, not the switchboard.

What the Shapley and one-order steps say

  • The net fiscal effect falls from $106.8B at the BSZ end to −$39.0B at the RJKDC end, a gap of $145.8B. The three largest Shapley steps are one-time tax estimated as a permanent 5 pp rate (−$33.6B, scenario + guesswork); billionaires already departed (−$33.2B, guesswork on data); how long the income tax loss lasts (−$27.1B, scenario).
  • The horizon is the single largest step read in Hoopes's order (−$66.5B, the last switch, when the loss is already RJKDC-sized) but only −$27.1B averaged over orders, because a perpetuity multiplies whatever annual loss the other inputs imply. The annual loss runs from $0.15B (BSZ) to $2.05B (RJKDC, September 2026).
  • One step raises the estimate: the RJKDC analysis applies no avoidance or evasion allowance beyond migration (its semi-elasticity is the migration share of Brulhart et al.'s response), so dropping the BSZ analysis's 10% adds +$11.2B (Shapley). DISPUTES.md does not list this as a row.
  • Order matters most for estimating the one-time tax as a permanent 5-percentage-point rate: −$33.6B averaged over all orders and −$11.1B in the one order shown. The RJKDC revenue is uniform between a confirmed-departures ceiling and an elasticity floor, so the elasticity bites only on top of whatever departures are already in. The step is large when switched early and smaller when switched after the departures.
  • Model differences are small and shown, not hidden: +$0.10B, from the rounded literals in the RJKDC Monte Carlo code (MISMATCHES #6 in rjkdc-analysis).
  • The September 2026 revision is the last step, −$14.3B. The RJKDC analysis prints its mean NPV as −$38.9B, from one seeded Monte Carlo run; the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, the bridge's RJKDC end, is −$38.98B.
Notes and data

Where the numbers come from. Every number on this page is computed by comparison/R/ from the two analyses' export contracts (bsz-analysis/export/r/, rjkdc-analysis/export/r/: the files each analysis's pipeline writes for others to read) and the page-referenced transcriptions in comparison/data/. A Python twin reproduces them (R and Python agree to 1e-9 on every exported number; see comparison/tests). The reproduction report derives each one step by step, and the materials page is a guide to the code, tests and files.

Data. bridge.csv (every step, for each output and horizon setting), ends.csv (the two ends of every bridge), switchboard.csv (the value for every mix of switches: bit i of mask is the i-th switch, in the bridge's order), inputs.csv (every disputed input and its sources), comparison-data.js (what this page reads). The pipeline writes all of them; a test fails if comparison-data.js or this page differs from its output.

Links. The settings can be set from the address, for example #method=sequential&h=common_20.
