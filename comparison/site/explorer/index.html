Comparison of the two analyses of California Proposition 40: Open Output (here) · Open Analysis: Report · Open Materials · Overview

Why do the two analyses of Proposition 40 disagree?

The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, August 2026) finds that the one-time billionaire tax raises money for California, net of the income tax it changes. The RJKDC analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026) finds that it loses money, because it counts the income tax lost to billionaires who leave for ever. This page puts both on one definition, the net fiscal effect, and lists the assumptions the two analyses disagree on in two panels. The BSZ panel starts from the BSZ analysis's values and the RJKDC panel from the RJKDC analysis's. Each slider moves one assumption part of the way, or all the way, from one analysis's value to the other's. The bars between the panels show what one estimating function, used for both analyses, returns for each panel.

Two panels, one estimating function

Each panel lists every assumption the two analyses disagree on (this page calls them the disputed inputs), plus the RJKDC analysis's September 2026 revision. The BSZ panel on the left starts every slider at the BSZ analysis's value. The RJKDC panel on the right starts every slider at the RJKDC analysis's value. A slider has five positions: its own analysis's value, the other analysis's value, and three evenly spaced steps between them. For the loss horizon the five positions are 5, 10, 20 and 50 years, and a perpetuity. In both panels the BSZ value is printed at a slider's left end and the RJKDC value at its right end. The filled dot is where the slider is now, the hollow dot the other analysis's value, and the value under the filled dot is the one in use.

The bars in the middle show what the common estimating function returns for each panel. That function, score_common(), computes both analyses: every disputed input is one of its arguments, and it returns the net fiscal effect. The two panels are independent. The left sliders move only the left bar, and the right sliders only the right bar. Move every slider in the BSZ panel to its RJKDC end and the left bar meets the right one; the reverse works too. Your browser does the arithmetic with model.js, a copy of the function the pipeline uses (comparison/R/model.R). A test checks that the copy returns the pipeline's values.

The September 2026 revision slider. The RJKDC values on the other sliders are those of the RJKDC analysis's March 2026 version (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso, SSRN), the last version whose inputs are published. The September 2026 revision (Jaros and Rauh, NBER) changes things the common function cannot take as inputs. It weights the revenue by the chance that the tax survives litigation. It grows the base 7% to the end of 2026. It also draws the share of billionaires who leave independently of the revenue. So this slider has two positions, not five. When it is on, it adds the revision step plus the model difference. The model difference is the small gap between the common function at the March 2026 inputs and that version's own Monte Carlo, which uses rounded numbers. Both depend on the horizon, and each panel adds them at its own horizon slider's setting. At a perpetuity they are −$14.3B and +$0.10B.

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax (Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the present value of the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted for 5 years and discounted at 3%. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The RJKDC analysis's is a perpetuity net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER c15504, September 2026).

The net fiscal effect puts both on one definition, and the two panels start at its two ends. The BSZ end is the same $106.8B, with the 5 years discounted over the RJKDC analysis's 1.5% to 4.5% rate range instead of at 3%. The RJKDC end is −$39.0B, the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, of which the authors' seeded run gives −$38.9B.

The bridge: how much each assumption moves the estimate

The panels show one mix of slider positions at a time. The bridge below adds up the whole move, from the BSZ end to the RJKDC end, as a waterfall chart. The BSZ end is $106.8B and the RJKDC end −$39.0B, a gap of $145.8B. Each bar between the two ends is the effect of moving one assumption all the way from the BSZ value to the RJKDC value. The last two bars belong to the revision slider: the model difference and the September 2026 revision. Together the bars add up to −$145.8B.

The bridge uses the same estimating function, score_common() (comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin and the browser copy model.js). With every input at the BSZ value the function returns the BSZ end exactly. With every input at the RJKDC value it returns the Monte Carlo expectation of the RJKDC version those values come from (the note on the revision slider says which), up to a $0.10B model difference. The bridge runs in the direction of Figure 2 of Hoopes (2026), the existing comparison described on the overview.

What a step adds depends on which other inputs have already moved, because the inputs interact: a departure matters more when the base is larger. So the bars can be sized two ways. The default bars are Shapley values: each step's effect averaged over all 39,916,800 orders in which the inputs could be moved, so the bars add up exactly to the gap whatever the reading order. "One order" shows a single sequence instead, the one Hoopes reads in. The bridge uses each analysis's own horizon (5 years for the BSZ analysis, a perpetuity for the RJKDC analysis), so the horizon is one of its steps. This setting changes the bridge only, not the panels.

What the Shapley and one-order steps say

  • The net fiscal effect falls from $106.8B at the BSZ end to −$39.0B at the RJKDC end, a gap of $145.8B. The three largest Shapley steps are one-time tax estimated as a permanent 5 pp rate (−$33.6B, scenario + guesswork); billionaires already departed (−$33.2B, guesswork on data); how long the income tax loss lasts (−$27.1B, scenario).
  • The horizon is the single largest step read in Hoopes's order (−$66.5B, the last step, when the loss is already RJKDC-sized) but only −$27.1B averaged over orders, because a perpetuity multiplies whatever annual loss the other inputs imply. The annual loss runs from $0.15B (BSZ) to $2.05B (RJKDC, September 2026).
  • One step raises the estimate: the RJKDC analysis applies no avoidance or evasion allowance beyond migration (its semi-elasticity is the migration share of Brulhart et al.'s response), so dropping the BSZ analysis's 10% adds +$11.2B (Shapley). DISPUTES.md does not list this as a row.
  • Order matters most for estimating the one-time tax as a permanent 5-percentage-point rate: −$33.6B averaged over all orders and −$11.1B in the one order shown. The RJKDC revenue is uniform between a confirmed-departures ceiling and an elasticity floor, so the elasticity bites only on top of whatever departures are already in. The step is large when it is taken early and smaller when it comes after the departures.
  • Model differences are small and shown, not hidden: +$0.10B, from the rounded literals in the RJKDC Monte Carlo code (MISMATCHES #6 in rjkdc-analysis).
  • The September 2026 revision is the last step, −$14.3B. The RJKDC analysis prints its mean NPV as −$38.9B, from one seeded Monte Carlo run; the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, the bridge's RJKDC end, is −$38.98B.
Notes and data

Where the numbers come from. Every number on this page is computed by comparison/R/ from the two analyses' export contracts (bsz-analysis/export/r/, rjkdc-analysis/export/r/: the files each analysis's pipeline writes for others to read) and the page-referenced transcriptions in comparison/data/. A Python twin reproduces them (R and Python agree to 1e-9 on every exported number; see comparison/tests). The panels' values are computed in the browser by model.js from the inputs in the data file; a test runs it with node over 600 slider mixes and checks it against the R values to 1e-9. The reproduction report derives each number step by step, and the materials page is a guide to the code, tests and files.

Data. bridge.csv (every step, for each output and horizon setting), ends.csv (the two ends of every bridge, and the two constants the revision slider adds), slider_sample.csv (300 slider mixes per panel with their values, the sample the tests run model.js on), switchboard.csv (the value for every mix of inputs at one analysis's value or the other's, which the Shapley values average over: bit i of mask is the i-th input, in the bridge's order), inputs.csv (every disputed input and its sources), comparison-data.js (what this page reads). The pipeline writes all of them; a test fails if comparison-data.js or this page differs from its output.

Links. The bridge's setting can be set from the address, for example #method=sequential.
