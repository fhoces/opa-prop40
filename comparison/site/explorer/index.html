Comparison of the two analyses of California Proposition 40: Open Output (here) · Open Analysis: Report · Open Materials · Overview

Why do the two analyses of Proposition 40 disagree?

The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, August 2026) finds that the one-time billionaire tax raises money for California, net of the income tax it changes. The RJKDC analysis (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026) finds that it loses money once income tax lost to billionaires who leave is counted for ever. Here both are put on one definition, the net fiscal effect, and the bridge below walks from one to the other, one disputed input at a time. Every number is precomputed by the pipeline that reproduces both.

Outcomes

The two ends of the bridge, output (b) net fiscal effect, each analysis's own horizon

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax ($103.8B, Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the present value of the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted for 5 years and discounted at 3%. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The RJKDC analysis's is a perpetuity net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER c15504, September 2026).

The common output (b), the net fiscal effect, puts both on one definition, and the two bars are its two ends. The BSZ end is the same $106.8B, with the 5 years discounted over the RJKDC analysis's 1.5% to 4.5% rate range instead of at 3%. The RJKDC end is −$39.0B, the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, of which the authors' seeded run gives −$38.9B.

The bridge, one disputed input at a time

On the common output (b), the BSZ end is $106.8B and the RJKDC end −$39.0B: a gap of $145.8B. The waterfall walks it from left to right. Every bar between the two ends is a step of that gap: one per disputed input, then the model difference and the September 2026 revision. Together they add up to −$145.8B. On the secondary output (a), the gross revenue, the ends are $103.8B and $36.0B, a gap of $67.8B.

One estimating function, score_common() (comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin), estimates both analyses. Every disputed input is an argument that takes either the BSZ value or the RJKDC value. With every input at the BSZ value it returns the BSZ end exactly (on (a), Table 5 row 1, $103.815B). The RJKDC values are those of its March 2026 version, where the inputs are published. With every input at those values the function returns that version's Monte Carlo expectation up to a $0.10B model difference on (b), shown as its own step. The September 2026 revision is the last step, to the RJKDC end. The bridge runs in the direction of Figure 2 of Hoopes (2026), the existing comparison described on the overview.

Because the inputs interact (a departure matters more when the base is larger), the size of a step depends on the order the inputs are switched. The default bars are Shapley values: each step's switch averaged over all 39,916,800 possible orders, so the steps add up exactly to the gap whatever the reading order. "One order" shows a single sequence instead, the one Hoopes reads in.

What the Shapley and one-order steps say

  • Output (b), the net fiscal effect, falls from $106.8B at the BSZ end to −$39.0B at the RJKDC end, a gap of $145.8B. The three largest Shapley steps are one-time tax estimated as a permanent 5 pp rate (−$33.6B, scenario + guesswork); billionaires already departed (−$33.2B, guesswork on data); how long the income tax loss lasts (−$27.1B, scenario).
  • For output (b) the horizon is the single largest step read in Hoopes's order (−$66.5B, the last switch, when the loss is already RJKDC-sized) but only −$27.1B averaged over orders, because a perpetuity multiplies whatever annual loss the other inputs imply. The annual loss runs from $0.15B (BSZ) to $2.05B (RJKDC, September 2026).
  • Output (a), the gross revenue, falls from $103.8B to $36.0B, a gap of $67.8B. The three largest Shapley steps are billionaires already departed (−$21.3B, guesswork on data); one-time tax estimated as a permanent 5 pp rate (−$21.1B, scenario + guesswork); base list and valuation date (−$9.4B, data).
  • One step raises both outputs: the RJKDC analysis applies no avoidance or evasion allowance beyond migration (its semi-elasticity is the migration share of Brulhart et al.'s response, March 2026 version pp.13-14), so dropping the BSZ analysis's 10% adds +$11.2B on (b) and +$8.0B on (a) (Shapley). DISPUTES.md does not list this as a row.
  • Order matters most for estimating the one-time tax as a permanent 5-percentage-point rate: on (b), −$33.6B averaged over all orders and −$11.1B in the one order shown (on (a), −$21.1B and −$10.2B). The RJKDC revenue is uniform between a confirmed-departures ceiling and an elasticity floor, so the elasticity bites only on top of whatever departures are already in. The step is large when switched early and smaller when switched after the departures.
  • Model differences are small and shown, not hidden: +$0.10B on (b) and −$0.0003B on (a), from the rounded literals in the March 2026 version's code (MISMATCHES #6 in rjkdc-analysis).
  • The September 2026 revision is the last step: −$14.3B on (b) and −$15.3B on (a). The RJKDC analysis prints its mean NPV as −$38.9B, from one seeded Monte Carlo run; the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, the bridge's RJKDC end, is −$38.98B.
Notes and data

Where the numbers come from. Every number on this page is computed by comparison/R/ from the two analyses' export contracts (bsz-analysis/export/r/, rjkdc-analysis/export/r/: the files each analysis's pipeline writes for others to read) and the page-referenced transcriptions in comparison/data/. A Python twin reproduces them (R and Python agree to 1e-9 on every exported number; see comparison/tests). The reproduction report derives each one step by step, and the materials page is a guide to the code, tests and files.

Data. bridge.csv (every step, for each output and horizon setting), ends.csv (the two ends of every bridge), inputs.csv (every disputed input and its sources), comparison-data.js (what this page reads). The pipeline writes all of them; a test fails if comparison-data.js or this page differs from its output.

Links. The settings can be set from the address, for example #out=a&method=sequential&h=common_20.
