Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Reconciliation of the Two Analyses

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax Californians worth $1 billion or more once, at 5% of their net worth, paid over five years. The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, NBER Working Paper 35218, August 2026) finds that it raises {{hl_bsz}} net of the income tax it changes. The JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026) finds that it leaves the state {{nber_npv_abs}} poorer, because billionaires who leave stop paying California income tax for ever. This page puts both on one definition and shows which assumptions make the difference. It follows the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework.

BSZ main estimate1 {{hl_bsz}} Net total: the wealth tax plus the income tax it changes, over 5 years, in today's dollars
JR main estimate1 {{hl_rjkdc}} Net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, in today's dollars, averaged over the authors' model runs (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026)

Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

A common output definition

one definition both numbers can be put on, and the conventions behind it

The two headlines answer different questions: the BSZ analysis reports what the tax collects, the JR analysis a net present value that subtracts income tax lost for ever. Both are put on two outputs. The explorer shows (b); (a) is in the report.

(b) net fiscal effect = (a) + extra income tax from assets sold to pay - present value of income tax lost to departures, over a horizon of H years (a) gross revenue = one-time wealth tax collected

The horizon H is itself disputed: about the 5 payment years for the BSZ analysis, for ever for the JR analysis. So it is a dial in the explorer and a step of the bridge. Present values use the JR analysis's discount-rate range, {{r_min}} to {{r_max}}; the BSZ analysis takes no position on the rate.

Conventions. Nominal billions of dollars. The wealth tax is counted at face value when assessed and not discounted over the five installments, as both analyses do. The BSZ analysis values the base on 1 July 2026; the JR analysis grows its 2025 Forbes base by 7% to 31 December 2026. The JR code labels its rate draw r − g and its text r (the JR analysis's mismatch 3).

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses, and so this OPA, take the measure as written (docs/initiative/). Neither models the day-to-day movement of the Forbes base. Nothing is added to either analysis here.

The existing comparison: Hoopes (2026)

the one published comparison, read off its chart and set beside this bridge

Jeffrey Hoopes, "Galle v Rauh: Comparing Two Revenue Estimates for California Billionaire Taxation" (SSRN 6428578), is the one published comparison. It compares earlier versions: the February 2026 GGSS expert report (Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske), on which the BSZ analysis builds, and the March 2026 version of the JR analysis (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso). His Figure 2 prints no values, so his column is read off the chart (tick fit within {{hoopes_fit}}, about {{hoopes_unc}} per bar; comparison/tools/digitize_hoopes_fig2.py). Beside it is our bridge in his order, ending at the September 2026 version.

Disclosures from the note itself: Hoopes states his priors, "To lay my cards on the table, I believe that the world is complicated, and that when possible, empirical analysis should be done in as detailed a way as possible" (p.2), and thanks "David Gamage for encouraging me to read both of these analyses carefully and compare the two" (p.1); Gamage is a GGSS co-author.

1 Each analysis's own main estimate. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years at 3%: the wealth tax ({{bsz_row1}}; the paper prints {{bsz_printed}}, Table 5 row 1), plus the income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the income tax lost to those who leave. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice (BSZ analysis overview). The JR analysis's is a net present value in perpetuity, averaged over the authors' 100,000 Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER chapter c15504, Tax Policy and the Economy vol. 41, September 2026). On the common output the BSZ end is the same {{bsz_end_b}}, discounted over the {{r_min}} to {{r_max}} range; the JR end is {{nber_end_b}}, the exact mean of those draws, of which the authors' seeded run gives {{hl_rjkdc}}.
