Why do the two analyses of Proposition 40 disagree?

The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, August 2026) finds that the one-time billionaire tax raises money for California, net of the income tax it changes. The JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026, a working paper of the National Bureau of Economic Research, NBER) finds that it loses money, because it counts the income tax lost to billionaires who leave for ever. This page puts both on one definition, the net fiscal effect. Each slider moves one disputed assumption from one analysis's value towards the other's. The bars show what one estimating function returns for each panel.

BSZ main estimate1 {{hl_bsz}} Net total over 5 years: the wealth tax plus the income tax it changes, in today's dollars
JR main estimate1 {{hl_rjkdc}} Net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, in today's dollars, averaged over the authors' model runs

Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

Two panels, one estimating function

Each panel holds the assumptions the two analyses disagree on, plus the JR analysis's September 2026 revision. The BSZ panel starts every slider at the BSZ value, the JR panel at the JR value. A slider has five positions: the two analyses' values and three steps between them. The BSZ value is printed at the left end and the JR value at the right end. Hover over a slider, or tap it, for what it changes and why the two analyses differ. Three inputs that barely move the estimate (non-US-citizen residents, the real-estate deduction, extra income tax from asset sales) share one switch.

The bars show what the common estimating function returns for each panel. The left sliders move only the left bar and the right sliders only the right bar. Move every slider in one panel to its far end and the two bars meet. The report states the function line by line; your browser runs a copy of it, which a test checks against the pipeline.

Starts at {{nber_end_b2}}, the exact average of the authors' Monte Carlo simulation (a model run many times with its uncertain inputs drawn at random). The authors' own run of it gives {{hl_rjkdc}}, the main estimate in the header; the two differ by sampling error.

The September 2026 revision switch. The JR values on the sliders are those of the JR analysis's March 2026 version (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso, SSRN), the version whose inputs the common function takes one by one. The September 2026 revision changes things the function cannot take as inputs. It weights the revenue by the chance that the tax survives litigation, grows the base {{nber_growth}} to the end of 2026, and draws the share of billionaires who leave independently of the revenue. So the switch is off or on. On, it adds the revision step plus the small model difference, both at the panel's horizon setting. At a perpetuity they are {{rev_step_b}} and {{rev_model_b}}.

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax (Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, for 5 years at 3%. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The JR analysis's is a perpetuity net present value, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER chapter c15504, Tax Policy and the Economy vol. 41, September 2026).

The net fiscal effect puts both on one definition, and the panels start at its two ends. The BSZ end is {{bsz_end_b}} and the JR end {{nber_end_b}}, a gap of {{gap_b}}. The BSZ end discounts the same 5 years over the JR analysis's {{r_min}} to {{r_max}} rate range instead of at 3%. The JR end is the exact average of the Monte Carlo whose own run gives {{hl_rjkdc}}.

Conventions. All amounts are nominal billions of dollars. The wealth tax is counted at face value when assessed and undiscounted, as both analyses do (the BSZ analysis values the base on 1 July 2026; the JR analysis grows its 2025 Forbes base by {{nber_growth}} to 31 December 2026). Present values are at the assessment date. The {{r_min}} to {{r_max}} range is what the JR code draws; the code labels it r − g and the text r (the JR analysis's mismatch 3).

Notes and data

Where the numbers come from. Every number on this page is computed by comparison/R/ from the two analyses' export contracts (bsz-analysis/export/r/, rjkdc-analysis/export/r/: the files each pipeline writes for others to read) and the transcriptions in comparison/data/. The estimating function is score_common() in comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin ({{parity_note}}). The browser runs model.js, a copy of it, and a test runs that copy with node over {{n_slider_sample}} slider mixes against the R values, to 1e-9. The report derives each number step by step and shows the bridge from the BSZ end to the JR end; the materials page lists the code, tests and files.

Data. ends.csv (the two ends and the two constants the revision switch adds), slider_sample.csv ({{n_slider_per_panel}} slider mixes per panel with their values, the sample the tests run model.js on), inputs.csv (every disputed input and its sources), bridge.csv (the bridge, step by step), comparison-data.js (what this page reads). A test fails if comparison-data.js or this page differs from the generator's output.
