Comparison of the two analyses of California Proposition 40: Open Output (here) · Open Analysis: Report · Open Materials · Overview

Why do the two analyses of Proposition 40 disagree?

The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, August 2026) finds that the one-time billionaire tax raises money for California, net of the income tax it changes. The RJKDC analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026; NBER is the National Bureau of Economic Research, which circulates working papers) finds that it loses money, because it counts the income tax lost to billionaires who leave for ever. This page puts both on one definition, the net fiscal effect, and lists the assumptions the two analyses disagree on in two panels. The BSZ panel starts from the BSZ analysis's values and the RJKDC panel from the RJKDC analysis's. Each slider moves one assumption part of the way, or all the way, from one analysis's value to the other's. The bars between the panels show what one estimating function, used for both analyses, returns for each panel.

BSZ main estimate1 {{hl_bsz}} Net total: the wealth tax plus the income tax it changes, over 5 years, in today's dollars
RJKDC main estimate1 {{hl_rjkdc}} Net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, in today's dollars, averaged over the authors' model runs (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026)

Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

Two panels, one estimating function

Each panel lists every assumption the two analyses disagree on (this page calls them the disputed inputs), plus the RJKDC analysis's September 2026 revision. The BSZ panel on the left starts every slider at the BSZ analysis's value. The RJKDC panel on the right starts every slider at the RJKDC analysis's value. A slider has five positions: its own analysis's value, the other analysis's value, and three evenly spaced steps between them. For the loss horizon the five positions are {{hz_words}}. In both panels the BSZ value is printed at a slider's left end and the RJKDC value at its right end. The filled dot is where the slider is now, the hollow dot the other analysis's value, and the value under the filled dot is the one in use.

The bars in the middle show what the common estimating function returns for each panel. One formula, used for both analyses, computes both: every disputed input is one of its arguments, and it returns the net fiscal effect. The two panels are independent. The left sliders move only the left bar, and the right sliders only the right bar. Move every slider in the BSZ panel to its RJKDC end and the left bar meets the right one; the reverse works too. Your browser does the arithmetic with a copy of the pipeline's function, and a test checks that the copy returns the pipeline's values (the names and files are under Notes and data).

In words, the two parts of the formula that are not obvious: the RJKDC revenue is read as the midpoint of a range between a ceiling (the base left after the confirmed departures) and a floor (the base left after the elasticity response), so the permanent-rate and elasticity sliders act only through that floor; and the share of billionaire income tax lost combines the BSZ movers' share with the RJKDC proportional loss. The report states the whole function line by line.

Every slider starts at the RJKDC value, so the panel starts at {{nber_end_b2}}, the exact expectation of the authors' Monte Carlo simulation. In a Monte Carlo simulation the authors draw each uncertain input at random many times, 100,000 here, and average the result; the exact expectation is that average computed by formula rather than by drawing. Their seeded run gives {{hl_rjkdc}}, the main estimate in the header: one run of those random draws started from a fixed seed number (2026), so it gives the same draws every time; its mean differs from the exact expectation by sampling error.

The September 2026 revision slider. The RJKDC values on the other sliders are those of the RJKDC analysis's March 2026 version (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso, SSRN, a preprint site), the version whose inputs the common function can take one by one. The September 2026 revision (Jaros and Rauh, NBER; its code and person-level file are in the same public repository) changes things the common function cannot take as inputs. It weights the revenue by the chance that the tax survives litigation. It grows the base {{nber_growth}} to the end of 2026. It also draws the share of billionaires who leave independently of the revenue. So this slider has two positions, not five. When it is on, it adds the revision step plus the model difference. The model difference is the small gap between the common function at the March 2026 inputs and that version's own Monte Carlo, which uses rounded numbers. Both depend on the horizon, and each panel adds them at its own horizon slider's setting. At a perpetuity they are {{rev_step_b}} and {{rev_model_b}}.

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax (Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the present value of the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted for 5 years and discounted at 3%. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The RJKDC analysis's is a perpetuity net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (Jaros and Rauh, NBER c15504, September 2026).

The net fiscal effect puts both on one definition, and the two panels start at its two ends. The BSZ end is the same {{bsz_end_b}}, with the 5 years discounted over the RJKDC analysis's {{r_min}} to {{r_max}} rate range instead of at 3%. The RJKDC end is {{nber_end_b}}, the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, of which the authors' seeded run gives {{hl_rjkdc}}.

Conventions. All amounts are nominal billions of dollars. The wealth tax is counted at its face value when assessed (the BSZ analysis values the base on 1 July 2026; the RJKDC analysis's September version grows the base it used in March, the 2025 Forbes list, by {{nber_growth}} to 31 December 2026), undiscounted although the Act collects it in five yearly installments; both analyses do this, so the comparison follows them. Present values are at the assessment date. The {{r_min}} to {{r_max}} range is what the RJKDC code draws; its code labels the draw (r − g), the rate less the growth of the lost income tax, and its text r (the RJKDC analysis's mismatch 3).

The bridge: how much each assumption moves the estimate

The panels show one mix of slider positions at a time. The bridge below adds up the whole move, from the BSZ end to the RJKDC end, as a waterfall chart. The BSZ end is {{bsz_end_b}} and the RJKDC end {{nber_end_b}}, a gap of {{gap_b}}. Each bar between the two ends is the effect of moving one assumption all the way from the BSZ value to the RJKDC value. The last two bars belong to the revision slider: the model difference and the September 2026 revision. Together the bars add up to {{gap_b_signed}}.

The bridge uses the same formula. With every input at the BSZ value the function returns the BSZ end exactly. With every input at the RJKDC value it returns the Monte Carlo expectation of the RJKDC version those values come from (the note on the revision slider says which), up to a {{rauh_resid_b}} model difference. The bridge runs in the direction of Figure 2 of Hoopes (2026), the existing comparison described on the overview.

What a step adds depends on which other inputs have already moved, because the inputs interact: a departure matters more when the base is larger. So the bars can be sized two ways. The default bars are Shapley values: each step's effect averaged over all {{n_orders}} orders in which the inputs could be moved, so the bars add up exactly to the gap whatever the reading order. Because the inputs interact, a Shapley bar is an average that includes each input's share of those interactions; it is not the effect of moving that input alone. "One order" shows a single sequence instead, the one Hoopes reads in, where each bar is that input's effect at its place in the sequence. The bridge uses each analysis's own horizon (5 years for the BSZ analysis, a perpetuity for the RJKDC analysis), so the horizon is one of its steps. This setting changes the bridge only, not the panels.

Notes and data

Where the numbers come from. Every number on this page is computed by comparison/R/ from the two analyses' export contracts (bsz-analysis/export/r/, rjkdc-analysis/export/r/: the files each analysis's pipeline writes for others to read) and the page-referenced transcriptions in comparison/data/. The estimating function is score_common() in comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin; the browser runs model.js, a copy of it. A Python twin reproduces them ({{parity_note}}). The panels' values are computed in the browser by model.js from the inputs in the data file; a test runs it with node over {{n_slider_sample}} slider mixes and checks it against the R values to 1e-9. The reproduction report derives each number step by step, and the materials page is a guide to the code, tests and files.

Data. bridge.csv (every step, for each output and horizon setting), ends.csv (the two ends of every bridge, and the two constants the revision slider adds), slider_sample.csv ({{n_slider_per_panel}} slider mixes per panel with their values, the sample the tests run model.js on), switchboard.csv (the value for every mix of inputs at one analysis's value or the other's, which the Shapley values average over: bit i of mask is the i-th input, in the bridge's order), inputs.csv (every disputed input and its sources), comparison-data.js (what this page reads). The pipeline writes all of them; a test fails if comparison-data.js or this page differs from its output.

Links. The bridge's setting can be set from the address, for example #method=sequential.
