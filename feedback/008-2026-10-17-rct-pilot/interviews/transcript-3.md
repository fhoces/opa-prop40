# Interview transcript: respondent 3 (explorer, treatment)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

1. I think the survey was to find out which value of each input experts find more believable, so the study can show which numbers hold up. It said the values should not depend on whether you support the tax. I answered from the choices, which is what it asked for.

2. The explorer page had a button saying "what do you think these values are?", so I expected to give my own number for each input. The survey gave two proposed values per question and asked which I found more credible. That was a different task, and it was easier, because I did not have to work out a figure myself.

3. It took about six minutes, which matched the "about 5 minutes" on the first page. The longest part was Q3 to Q5, the first input and its reasons, because I had to compare the two values on the page and think about each one.

4. I skipped the five smaller inputs (Q14 to Q18). Q13 said they had only a small effect on the estimate, so I chose "No, skip them." I also left the optional "Why?" boxes blank for Q8, Q12 and Q19, and I skimmed the long explanations under the choices, since I do not read long text in surveys.

5. I would shorten the explanations under each choice to one or two lines. The "Why?" boxes are fine as optional boxes, but the long text above them took the most time and I did not read it all. I would also say at the top which value is the one the explorer uses as its main estimate, so I would know which answer lines up with the headline numbers.

6. The "JR:" and "BSZ:" labels were the names of the two studies, and I took them that way from the explorer page. I did not know which study was for or against the tax beyond the headline results on that page, so I did not think of either value as coming from a side. Where I got the sense that one study was more pro-tax came from the explorer's headline numbers, and I'm not sure that sense was fair.

7. "About equally credible" meant I could not pick one value over the other. "Unsure" meant I did not know enough to judge. I chose "Unsure" in Q7 about billionaires' yearly California income tax, because I don't know how the state's tax data would be used to estimate that, and I left the reason blank.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

8. The values were not even-handed in wording. The JR option in Q9 calls its 50% a "guesstimated likelihood," and the BSZ option gives only "100%: the tax is collected in full, as written," so the JR value reads as the weaker one. In Q11 the JR option lists seven names and gives the 29% a full sentence, while the BSZ option adds caveats ("press reports do not by themselves establish a change of legal residence," "an aggressive scenario"). Both sides had some extra words, but the "guesstimated" in Q9 is the one that stood out to me.

9. In Q9 the two values were answers to the same question, a survival chance for the tax (100% against 50%), so I could weigh one against the other directly. In Q3 they were about the same thing but on different bases: 45% of all billionaires' income tax against 5% of the tax of the people who leave. I could compare them, but I had to think about which group each percentage was of. In Q11 the two values measure different things: JR's 29% is a count of seven named people's wealth, and BSZ's 5% is a general allowance with no names. Both are percentages of wealth, but one is a list and one is a guess at the rest.

10. The Q3 choice that fits that person best is option 2, "BSZ: 5%, only the income tax of those who leave." That matches "only the income tax of those who leave is lost" word for word. The 29% in Q11 is about wealth that moved in time, not the share of income tax lost, so it does not decide the Q3 answer. The person could hold both views, and the survey does not say they conflict.

11. The page told me that the gap between the two estimates is $145.8 billion, and that the income tax lost when people leave accounts for most of it ($81.3 billion, 56%). It also said the chance the tax survives court explains another 27%, and that the rest is small. The four bars and the two labels, BSZ and JR, were the two studies' estimates. I did not read every number, but that is what I took away.

12. I think the page affected Q3 and Q5, the income tax lost questions, because I had just seen that it was the biggest part of the gap. In Q4 I wrote that the 5% "assumes almost none of them do," which I think came from that bar. The BSZ and JR labels did not change any answer by themselves, since I only used them as the names of the two studies. I did not use the page at all for Q9 or Q11, and I'm not sure how much the bars shaped my choices overall.

## Follow-up

1. The JR version says the loss is a flat 45% whatever share of billionaires leaves, which sounds like the tax lost does not depend on who actually moves. The BSZ version ties the loss to the wealth that leaves, 5% if 5% leaves and 29% if 29% does, which sounds like it follows real departures. I would pick BSZ now. My original Q3 answer was JR, because I thought rich people move when taxes rise and the 5% assumes almost none do. The new BSZ wording makes that reason less convincing to me, so the answer changed.

2. In Q9 the JR text now names an "accounting rule" and a "more likely than not" threshold, which sounds official and gives a reason. The BSZ text says the Act was "argued to have been drafted to stay clear of constitutional challenges," which also gives a reason, but it reads like a defence someone is making. In Q11 the BSZ text explains why no names are counted: a press report is not a record of a change of legal residence. The JR text lists the seven names and says they "are counted as having moved in time" but does not say why that counts. So to me the BSZ version of Q11 is the one that is explained more fully, and the JR version of Q9 sounds more authoritative.

3. The JR version is described more fully. It gives the range, $3.3 billion to $5.8 billion, and the two assumptions behind the ends (212 highest earners or spread among 500 filers). The BSZ version has one reason, that the wealthiest have about half the income of the highest earners. The JR text reads as more worked out, and that made it seem more careful to me. I'm not sure whether that comes from the words or from the range itself, but I would have trusted it more.
