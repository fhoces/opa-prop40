# Interview transcript: respondent 71 (explorer, control)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

Respondent 71, interview answers (in role):

1. It was asking which of two values for each part of the Proposition 40 estimate looked more believable to me, and why. I was supposed to pick one per input and give a reason if I wanted to.

2. The explorer said this was for economists and policy analysts and that the values should not depend on whether you support the tax. I expected a short set of questions about those numbers. The survey was mostly that, but it ran far longer than the "about 5 minutes" it promised, and the reasons under each choice were long.

3. It felt long. I spent about 18 minutes. The first page of inputs took the most effort, because I read both reasons for each choice closely. I can't say which section took longest in minutes.

4. I left the free-text "Why?" blank for Q8, Q12 and Q19, since "Unsure" already said what I thought and I had nothing more to add. I left the email question blank. I didn't skim the five optional inputs; I answered each one, but mostly with Unsure because I didn't know enough to judge.

5. I would shorten the choice texts. Each one carried figures I couldn't check (the 28.6% of wealth, the two Forbes lists), and I couldn't tell which inputs mattered most to the final number. I would also make the time estimate match the real length.

6. As far as I could tell, A and B were just the order of the options. From the explorer I guessed they tracked the two analyses, but the A side didn't stay on one analysis (A was the 45% and "forever" side in Q3 and Q5, but the 100% side in Q9), so my guess was weak. The survey never named who proposed either value, so I had no firm idea.

7. "About equally credible" would have meant both reasons seemed sound to me, and I never chose it. "Unsure" meant I had no basis to choose. I chose Unsure on Q3, Q5, Q7, Q9, Q11 and Q14 to Q18. On Q9 I doubted the 50% figure because of the word "guesstimated", but I had no basis for 100% either.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

Respondent 71, answers to questions 8 to 10 (in role):

8. Mostly yes, but not in Q9. The word "guesstimated" in B ("a guesstimated likelihood that the tax will not survive court litigation") makes the 50% figure sound like a rough hunch, while A ("100%: the tax is collected in full, as written") gets no such word. In Q11, B adds "treated as an aggressive scenario" after the four named moves, which makes it sound extreme in a way A does not. In Q7, A is much longer, with the top and bottom of its range, while B gets a single short reason.

9. Q3 and Q9 are two answers to the same question. Q3 asks for a share of the same income tax under two rules, and Q9 asks for one chance that the tax survives, so I could weigh 45% against 5%, and 100% against 50%. Q11 is also a share of the same wealth, but the two values rest on different bases. A counts seven named people, and B counts an allowance for unnamed people with no names at all. So I could compare them as numbers, but I could not tell whether they were measuring the same group of people.

10. The B choice in Q3 ("5%, only the income tax of those who leave") fits that view best. The view says only leavers' income tax is lost, and B is the rule that counts only leavers. The 29% in Q11 is a share of wealth, though, not of income tax, so the survey gives no option that combines the two. I would be picking B for the rule and taking the 29% from Q11 as a separate fact.

## Follow-up

Respondent 71, answers to the last three questions (in role):

1. Choice A says 45% of all billionaires' income tax is lost whoever leaves, and the 45% is set above the wealth that has left so it covers moves nobody reports. Choice B says only the income tax of leavers is lost, scaled to how much wealth leaves, so 5% or 29% of the wealth gives that share of their tax. I would pick B now, because it ties the loss to the people who actually leave. My original answer was Unsure, and I still am not sure how much of the tax leaves, so the pick is a rule I find more sensible, not a number I trust.

2. Yes, a little. In Q9, B's "more likely than not" comes from an accounting rule, which sounds official, while A's "argued to have been drafted" sounds like advocacy, so B reads as the more authoritative of the two. In Q11, the new B gives a reason ("a press report of a move is not a record of a change of legal residence"), while A gives only the list of names, so B sounds more principled even though it is the smaller number. The reason stays unstated in A.

3. Yes. A now says where the range comes from ("depending on whether the billionaires are taken to be the 212 highest earners or spread among the top 500 filers"), so it reads as carefully sourced. B's "halved" sounds like an arbitrary cut, even with the reason after it, so B reads as the weaker one of the two. That is the same kind of difference as before, but the new A makes it stronger.
