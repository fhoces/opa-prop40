# Respondent 64: city council member in a mid-sized California city, 30s, lives in California
Politics: moderate; undecided about wealth taxes. Knowledge: knows the wealth-tax literature well (Saez, Zucman, Rauh, Brulhart). Reading style: reads everything carefully.
Entry path: landing. Arm: control. Minutes: 24. Would leave an email: False.

## Their answers
- Q1. Do you consider yourself an expert in tax or fiscal policy? (required)
  Answer: Somewhat
- Q2. Before opening this survey, had you looked at news sources, web pages or academic studies comparing estimates of Proposition 40, the Billionaire Wealth Tax Proposal in California? (required)
  Answer: Yes
- Q3. The share of billionaires' income tax lost when people leave: which is more credible? (required)
  Answer: About equally credible
- Q4. Why? (optional)
  Answer: Both are plausible. I am not sure which group the 5% figure is measured on, so I cannot tell whether the two numbers are about the same people.
- Q5. How long the lost income tax is counted: which is more credible? (required)
  Answer: B. 5 years, about the payment period, treating the responses as temporary.
- Q6. Why? (optional)
  Answer: Some leavers will come back, but a five-year window fits a one-time tax with a five-year payment period. Treating the response as temporary seems more realistic to me.
- Q7. Billionaires' yearly California income tax: which is more credible? (required)
  Answer: A. $4.55 billion a year: the middle of a range from California tax data. The top, $5.8 billion, assumes the billionaires are the state's 212 highest earners. The bottom, $3.3 billion, assumes they are spread among the top 500 tax filers.
- Q8. Why? (optional)
  Answer: A gives a range from actual tax data and says which assumption sets each end. B rests on one research finding about the wealthiest, and the halving is a rough step.
- Q9. The chance the tax survives a court challenge: which is more credible? (required)
  Answer: Unsure
- Q10. Why? (optional)
  Answer: I cannot judge the court odds, so 50% is a guess in either direction and I would not rely on it.
- Q11. Billionaires who moved out of California before the 1 January 2026 residency date: which is more credible? (required)
  Answer: B. 5% of the wealth: an allowance for billionaires who managed to move in time. No named billionaire is counted, because press reports do not by themselves establish a change of legal residence. Counting four of them (Page, Thiel, Hankey and Kalanick) is treated as an aggressive scenario.
- Q12. Why? (optional)
  Answer: Press reports of a move are not the same as a change of legal residence. I would want residency filings before counting any name.
- Q13. The last five inputs have only a small, second-order effect on the estimate. Would you like to assess them anyway? (required)
  Answer: Yes, show me the five
- Q14. Wealth that escapes the tax through avoidance and evasion, beyond moving away: which is more credible? (optional)
  Answer: Unsure
- Q15. The billionaires' wealth the tax applies to: which is more credible? (optional)
  Answer: A. The Forbes list of 1 July 2026: 250 California billionaires worth $2,307 billion.
- Q16. Non-US-citizen residents: which is more credible? (optional)
  Answer: A. Taxed: the $150 billion of wealth of California billionaires who are not US citizens is in the base.
- Q17. Real-estate deduction: which is more credible? (optional)
  Answer: Unsure
- Q18. Extra income tax from assets sold to pay the tax: which is more credible? (optional)
  Answer: A. One third of the tax is paid by selling assets, and those gains are taxed.
- Q19. Why? (optional)
  Answer: Selling assets to pay the tax creates real gains, and not counting them seems to leave out a cost the tax itself creates.
- Q20. Is there an input missing from this list that you think matters? (optional)
  Answer: The date on which residency is measured, and how trusts and family entities holding wealth are treated.
- Q22. Do you support Proposition 40? (optional)
  Answer: Undecided

## Their out-of-character notes (not shown to the interviewer)
This respondent read the entry panel as a test of whether their inputs match the "right" values, and expected the results to show which analysis they agreed with. The landing message gave only the two headline numbers, so the BSZ and JR labels meant little until the respondent saw the explorer, which they had not yet read. They took BSZ as the $106.8B estimate and JR as the negative -$39.0B estimate, but did not know what the letters A and B stood for beyond the order of options on each question. The A and B options were hard to compare because the wording of each differs in length and detail, and the A option in Q7 is much longer than B. The respondent would likely have wanted to read the explorer page before answering, since the entry panel asked them to commit to values first.
