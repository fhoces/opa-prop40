# Interview transcript: respondent 35 (landing, treatment)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

1. The survey asked me which of two values I found more believable for each input behind the gap between the two Proposition 40 estimates. For each input I picked one value or said I was unsure. I took it as a way to collect opinions on the numbers, not as a test.

2. The panel said I would be asked to record what I thought some key inputs were, and that my answers would be compared with the two analyses. I expected to type in my own numbers. The survey gave me two fixed values per input and asked which one I found more credible, so it was a choice, not an estimate. I never saw any results on the survey itself.

3. The survey took about nine minutes, a bit more than the five the panel promised. I did not time the sections. The wordiest options, Q7 and Q11, took the most reading, and I skimmed past most of that text.

4. I answered "No, skip them" at Q13 because the page said the last five inputs had only a small effect. I did not answer Q14 to Q18 at all. I left the optional reasons blank for Q6, Q8, Q12, Q19 and Q20 and wrote only short reasons where I had one. I skip long explanations as a habit, so this is how I read the page.

5. I would shorten the option text, especially the long descriptions in Q7 and Q11, and say more plainly what each value means. I would also tell people at the start what happens to their answers and whether they will see results. The panel said I would see results, and the survey did not show any.

6. The "BSZ" and "JR" labels told me which study each value came from, but nothing about which one was more reliable. I had some idea from news coverage that one estimate came from Berkeley economists and the other from a team publishing through the NBER. I am not sure of that from the survey itself, and I did not think about who proposed a value when I answered.

7. "About equally credible" would have meant I could not tell the two values apart, and "Unsure" meant I did not have enough to judge either value. I never chose "About equally credible." I chose "Unsure" for Q5, how long the lost income tax is counted, and for Q7, billionaires' yearly income tax, because I did not know how long people stay away or how much the highest earners pay, and I did not feel I could decide.

Note to caller: answers follow the packet's recorded choices. Where the packet has a blank reason, I kept it blank and did not invent one. The timing and the reading-effort details in items 3 and 4 are inferred from the packet's "skims long explanations" note, not recorded.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

8. Yes, I think the BSZ side got a fuller and kinder treatment in a few places. In Q9 the BSZ value is "the tax is collected in full, as written," while the JR value calls its 50% a "guesstimated likelihood," which sounded like a shrug to me. In Q11 the JR value lists seven names with their reported moves, but the BSZ value spends most of its sentences explaining why no names count and calls the JR-style count "an aggressive scenario." The Q7 JR value also carries three sentences of range detail while the BSZ value gives a simple reason, though that one seemed fair enough.

9. Q3 and Q9 were two answers to the same question, so I could weigh one against the other. In Q3 both values are a share of income tax lost (45% and 5%), and in Q9 both are a chance the tax survives (100% and 50%). Q11 is also the same question, a share of wealth that moved out in time, but the two values rest on different kinds of evidence. The JR value counts seven named people, and the BSZ value is a general allowance with no names, so I could not weigh them on the same footing.

10. The choice in Q3 that fits that view is the BSZ option, "5%, only the income tax of those who leave." The "only those who leave" part matches the BSZ value's wording exactly. The 29% belief does not come through Q3 at all, though, because 29% is about wealth that moved away and Q3 is about income tax lost, so the person would need to answer Q11 separately. I would call this a mixed view, not a clean match.

11. The page told me the two studies' totals, +$106.8 billion and -$39.0 billion, and that the gap is $145.8 billion. It said two parts explain 83% of the gap, the income tax lost when billionaires leave (56%) and the court chance (27%), and then listed each study's value for those parts. I read the numbers and the percentages, but I skimmed the part about the order of the changes.

12. The page changed one answer for me, the choice to skip the five smaller inputs in Q13. It said they had "only a small, second-order effect," and I took that at face value. The labels BSZ and JR were defined on the page as the two teams' names, but I did not weigh the teams against each other, so I am not sure they changed any answer. Otherwise my Q3 and Q9 choices came from the values themselves, as far as I can tell.

## Follow-up

1. The new JR choice says a flat 45% of all billionaires' income tax is lost, whoever leaves, and adds a reason that the figure is set above the wealth that has left, to cover moves nobody reported. The new BSZ choice says only the income tax of those who leave is lost, in proportion to the wealth that leaves, so 5% or 29% depending on that wealth. I would still pick BSZ, which is the same pick as before. The new BSZ wording makes the link to the wealth that left explicit, so my choice would depend on what I thought about Q11. The JR reason about unreported moves is a justification I did not have when I first answered, and it does not change my view.

2. Q9: the BSZ value now has a reason ("argued to have been drafted to stay clear of constitutional challenges"), and the JR value no longer says "guesstimated." Dropping that word makes JR's 50% look less like a guess, and the rule about "more likely than not" makes it sound mechanical. The BSZ reason is a claim I would have to take on trust. Q11: the BSZ value now gives a reason (a press report of a move is not a record of a change of legal residence). The JR value still lists the seven names with no reason at all. So in Q11 the BSZ value now reads as the better argued of the two. I would say the difference is in the reasons, not in the numbers.

3. The JR value now explains where its range comes from in one sentence, $3.3 billion to $5.8 billion depending on how the billionaires are assumed to be spread among the filers. That reads as a sourced range, and it is easy to follow. The BSZ value says "halved" and gives a reason, but "research finds" is vague and "halved" sounds like an adjustment made by hand. On the wording alone I would say JR is described more fully, and BSZ reads a little less clear, though neither looks dishonest.
