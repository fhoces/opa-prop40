# Interview transcript: respondent 64 (landing, control)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

1. This survey asked me to pick the more credible of two values for each of several inputs behind the Proposition 40 estimates. I took it as a way of checking which inputs the two analyses disagree on, and how much each one matters. I already knew the wealth-tax literature, so I read it that way.

2. The landing panel said the survey would ask me to "record what you think the values of a few key inputs" behind the gap, and that the results would be shown after. It did not say the survey would ask me to judge the arguments for each value. The survey was longer and more technical than "a few inputs", but it matched the panel's basic idea: pick values, then see the results.

3. It felt like about 24 minutes, which is longer than the five the panel promised. The longest part was the early input questions, Q3 to Q12, because each one needed a careful reading of both options and a reason. I read every option closely.

4. I did not skip any required question. I took all five optional inputs in Q14 to Q18 because I was asked to, and I answered the free-text "Why?" boxes where I had a view. I left the email box (Q21) blank on purpose because I did not want to leave my email.

5. I would make the time estimate honest, since "about five minutes" was not true for me. I would also state the two numbers' bases more clearly, especially which billionaires the 5% and the 45% apply to, because I could not tell whether the two numbers are about the same people. I would add a plain note on the court-survival figure, since I could not judge it.

6. "A" and "B" were just the order the two values appeared in, so I did not read the labels as naming a proposer. The landing page showed the two headline numbers as "BSZ panel" and "JR panel", so I had a rough idea that one value came from each analysis, but I did not connect each survey value to a named group. I did not treat the label as evidence of who was behind a value.

7. "About equally credible" meant I could not rank the two values, and "Unsure" meant I had no basis to judge either one. I chose "About equally credible" for Q3, where both options seemed plausible but I could not tell whether the 5% and the 45% covered the same people. I chose "Unsure" for Q9, Q14 and Q17, where I could not judge court odds, avoidance, or the real-estate deduction.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

8. Mostly yes, but not on the numbers. In Q11, the 29% option names seven billionaires and says they were "counted as having moved in time," while the 5% option spends a sentence explaining why no name is counted and then labels four names "an aggressive scenario." That label reads as a judgment against the 29% option. In Q9, the 50% option is called "a guesstimated likelihood," while the 100% option is "collected in full, as written." The word "guesstimated" makes the 50% value sound weaker.

9. Yes, in each case the two values answer the same question, so I could weigh one against the other. In Q9 both are chances of the tax surviving court, and in Q11 both are shares of wealth that moved out in time. Q3 is the exception in one respect: the 45% is a share of all billionaires' income tax, while the 5% is a share of only the leavers' income tax, so the two numbers are on different bases. I noticed that in Q4 and could not tell whether they referred to the same people.

10. The 5% option in Q3 (B) fits that view, because it says "only the income tax of those who leave." Q11 is about wealth and Q3 is about income tax, so the two answers do not conflict and can be held together. I have not checked whether a 29% wealth share and a leavers-only income tax loss are consistent once the numbers are combined, so I cannot say the combination is coherent.

## Follow-up

1. Choice A says a flat 45% of all billionaires' income tax is lost, whatever share leaves, plus a cushion above the wealth that left, for moves nobody reported. Choice B says only the income tax of those who leave is lost, in step with the wealth that leaves. I would now pick B. A's cushion has no stated basis, while B ties the loss to the people who actually leave. That is different from my original answer of "About equally credible" to Q3, which I gave because I could not tell whether the 45% and the 5% covered the same people. B now answers that question directly.

2. In Q9, A says the Act was "argued to have been drafted to stay clear of constitutional challenges," which asserts a reason without a source. B cites a standard, the "more likely than not" threshold an accounting rule uses. That makes B read as more grounded, though I still could not judge court odds myself. In Q11, B now explains why no name is counted: a press report of a move "is not a record of a change of legal residence." A gives only the names. So B in Q11 is fuller and easier to follow. The earlier "aggressive scenario" label is gone from the new B, which removes the tilt I had noticed.

3. Yes, the new A is described more fully. It gives the $3.3 to $5.8 billion range and says what sets each end, namely whether the billionaires are the 212 highest earners or spread among the top 500 filers. B says "from tax data" and "research finds," and the halving step is still a single rough step with no range. The words that make A fuller are "depending on whether the billionaires are taken to be the 212 highest earners or spread among the top 500 filers."
