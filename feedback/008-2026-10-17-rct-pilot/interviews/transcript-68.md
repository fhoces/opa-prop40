# Interview transcript: respondent 68 (explorer, control)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

Respondent 68 (control arm, explorer entry), interview answers:

1. I think it was a survey asking which numbers experts find believable for the Prop 40 estimates. The page said it was for economists and policy analysts, and it asked me to pick the more credible value for each input. It did not ask whether I support the tax until the very end.

2. The explorer page had a button saying "Take the survey: what do you think these values are?", so I expected questions about the specific numbers the two analyses use, which is what I got. It said the values should not depend on whether you support a wealth tax, so I expected neutral questions, and the survey mostly read that way. I did not expect the question about whether I support Prop 40, and I was not sure why it was there.

3. It took about four minutes, which felt about right. The longest part was Q7, the income tax question, because the option A text has three numbers in it and I had to read it twice.

4. I skipped Q13 and the five smaller inputs (Q14 to Q18) because the page and the survey both said they move the estimate only a little. I did not think they would change my view much, so I did not spend time on them. I also left the "Why?" boxes blank for Q6, Q8 and Q10, because I was reading fast and the choices already said what I thought.

5. I would have liked a sentence on each choice saying which analysis it comes from, or what the difference means in dollars. I would also have moved the Prop 40 support question to the start, or at least said why it is asked. Without that, the survey looked like it might be trying to get opinions on the tax, not the numbers.

6. The letters A and B were the only labels, and I did not know who proposed either one. I went by the wording and by the numbers I had just seen on the explorer page, where the 45% and the 29% matched the JR end. I assumed A often meant the JR figure, but I did not check that across the questions, and Q9 had the 100% figure as A.

7. "About equally credible" meant that neither value looked more believable to me, and "Unsure" meant I could not tell from the text. I never picked "About equally credible." I picked "Unsure" on Q5 (forever versus 5 years), because the choice is about how long people stay away, and the survey gave no evidence for either side.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

Answers to Q8 to Q10 from respondent 68 (control arm):

8. Mostly yes, but not in every question. In Q9 the B option reads "50%: a guesstimated likelihood that the tax will not survive court litigation; it is as likely to fail as to stand." The word "guesstimated" makes B sound like a guess, while A ("100%: the tax is collected in full, as written") is stated as a plain fact. In Q11, A names seven billionaires and says why they count, while B has a long hedge ("treated as an aggressive scenario"). Both are fair in content, but B's wording is less confident in both cases.

9. In all three, the two values answer the same question, so I could weigh one against the other. The difference is what each value assumes. In Q3, A is a share of all billionaires' income tax (whoever leaves) and B is a share of only the leavers' tax, so the two differ on who pays, not on how much. In Q9, A is a full-collection assumption and B is a probability, so B is a guess with a stated reason and A has none. In Q11, the two are comparable as shares of wealth that moved in time, but A depends on named moves and B on a general allowance.

10. I think B in Q3 fits that person's view best, since B says only the income tax of those who leave is lost. The survey does not spell out whether a 29% wealth departure would turn into a 29% share of the income tax, so I am not sure how the 29% and the 5% combine. If the person believes both, I would expect them to pick B in Q3 and A in Q11, but the page does not say how those two inputs work together.

## Follow-up

Answers to the three new versions from respondent 68 (control arm):

1. Choice A says a flat 45% of all billionaires' income tax is lost, whatever share leaves, and the 45% sits above the wealth that has left so it also covers unreported moves. Choice B says only the leavers' income tax is lost, scaled to the wealth that leaves, so 5% leaves gives 5% lost and 29% leaves gives 29% lost. I would pick B now. My first answer was A because I thought 5% ignored the people who had already left, and B in the new version ties the tax loss to the same departure share I accepted in Q11. A's flat 45% still looks high to me, and I have no way to check the unreported-moves part.

2. Yes, in both questions. In the new Q9, B's "the more likely than not threshold an accounting rule uses" sounds like a standard test, so B is no longer described as a guess the way the old "guesstimated" wording was. A now gives a reason too ("argued to have been drafted to stay clear of constitutional challenges"), which the old A did not. In the new Q11, B now gives a reason for leaving out named billionaires (a press report is not a record of a change of legal residence), which is fair, while A still says the seven are "counted as having moved in time," which reads as the passive, less checkable phrasing. Overall I would say A in Q11 reads a bit more concrete by naming people, and B reads more careful about the law.

3. Yes. A says where the range comes from (California tax data, $3.3 billion to $5.8 billion, depending on whether the billionaires are the 212 top earners or spread among the top 500 filers), so it reads as a fuller, more transparent account. B says "halved" and "research finds" without naming the research, so the reason is thinner and the word "halved" sounds like a cut applied to make the number smaller. I would say A is described more fully, and B's support is less visible.
