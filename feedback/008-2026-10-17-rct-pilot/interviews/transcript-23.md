# Interview transcript: respondent 23 (explorer, treatment)

## Round 1 (questions 1 to 7)

1. I think the survey was asking which of two studies' numbers for Proposition 40 I found more believable, one input at a time. It was a check on the values behind the two estimates, not a poll on the tax. I had read the explorer page, so I understood the two studies were BSZ and JR.

2. The explorer page said the survey would ask what I thought these values were, and that an expert should be able to judge each one. The survey did that, with a short page explaining the $145.8 billion gap before the questions. I expected more time on the reasons behind each value, and the reason boxes were optional, so I left them blank.

3. It felt short, about four minutes, which matched the explorer's "about 5 minutes" roughly. The part that took longest was page 2, the breakdown of the gap, because I had to read the percentages before I could answer anything.

4. I skipped the five smaller inputs at Q13 because the survey itself said they only had a small, second-order effect. I also left all the optional "Why?" boxes empty. I skimmed the value text on most questions because I did not know enough about the tax data to weigh it.

5. I would make the reason boxes more prominent, or ask for one short reason on the questions where I answered Unsure. I would also say on the first page what "credible" means in practice, since I was not sure whether I was judging the method or the numbers. Without that, it was hard to pick between two sets of numbers I could not check.

6. The label in front of each value was the name of the study ("BSZ:" or "JR:"). I knew which study was which from the explorer page and the survey's second page, so the label meant "this is the Boll, Saez and Zucman number" or "this is the Jaros and Rauh number." I did not know anything about the authors' reputations, so I had no view on who was more trustworthy.

7. "About equally credible" meant I could not see one value as more believable than the other. "Unsure" meant I did not know enough to choose. I chose About equally credible on Q7 (the yearly income tax) because both ranges looked plausible to me and I could not tell which was closer. I chose Unsure on Q3, Q9 and Q11 (the share lost, the court chance and the early movers) because I did not know enough about those to pick one.

## Round 2 (questions 8 to 12)

8. The wording was not the same for the two values. The JR text in Q7 was longer and gave the ranges and assumptions (the 212 highest earners, the top 500 filers), while the BSZ text was one sentence. In Q9, BSZ's "collected in full, as written" was a plain statement, but JR's "a guesstimated likelihood" sounded like a guess that could be dismissed, and I noticed that word. I also read "aggressive scenario" in Q11 as a negative label on the BSZ side, though I'm not sure that was intended.

9. In Q3 and Q11 the two options answer the same question, the share lost, but they name different people. JR's option covers all billionaires, and BSZ's covers only those who leave, so I could not weigh one number directly against the other. Q9 was different in kind, because BSZ states a fact ("collected in full") and JR states a probability ("as likely to fail as to stand"). In Q11, the JR value names seven people while the BSZ value is a general allowance, so they are not the same type of claim.

10. I think the BSZ option in Q3 ("5%, only the income tax of those who leave") fits that person's view, since they believe only leavers' income tax is lost. Believing 29% moved in time does not change that, because Q3 is about how the lost tax is counted, not who moved. I'm not sure whether the 29% and the 5% interact in a way I would have seen on the page.

11. The page said the two estimates are $106.8 billion and minus $39.0 billion, a gap of $145.8 billion. It said the lost income tax explains most of the gap (about 56%), court survival about 27%, and billionaires counted as already gone about 21%. The other inputs together offset a little, about 3%. In short, it told me which inputs matter most for the disagreement.

12. I'm not sure the page changed any answer. It told me Q3 mattered most, so I paid attention to it, but I still answered Unsure there and skipped the reasons. The BSZ and JR labels helped me keep track of which value was which study's, and I did not change a choice because of them. The only answer I would say the page affected is Q5, where I picked BSZ's 5 years, though I can't say whether the page was the reason.

## Follow-up

1. JR's choice says that 45% of all billionaires' income tax is lost, whoever leaves, so the loss does not depend on how many leave. BSZ's choice says only the income tax of those who leave is lost, in proportion to the wealth that leaves. The BSZ version is easier for me to follow, because the loss rises and falls with the wealth that moves. I would pick BSZ now, which differs from my original Q3 answer of Unsure, though I am still not sure which is right.

2. In Q9, the BSZ text adds a reason ("argued to have been drafted to stay clear of constitutional challenges"), and that makes it sound better supported than JR's, which gives only the threshold a rule uses. In Q11, the JR text names seven people, which makes it concrete, while the BSZ text gives a reason for leaving out named people. I'd say the BSZ wording explains its choice more fully, but I can't say it is in a better light. The numbers in both are the only things I can compare directly.

3. The JR text is fuller. It gives the range, $3.3 to $5.8 billion, and says what each end assumes (212 highest earners or the top 500 filers). The BSZ text gives one number and a reason ("halved, because research finds"), which is shorter and sounds less checked to me. I would say the JR wording is the more detailed one, and that is the one I'd notice first.
