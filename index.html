California Proposition 40, Reconciliation of the Two Analyses

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax people who lived in California on 1 January 2026 and are worth $1 billion or more: once, at 5% of their whole net worth as valued on 31 December 2026, paid in five equal yearly installments. Two analyses estimate what it would bring in and reach very different numbers. The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, NBER Working Paper 35218, August 2026; NBER is the National Bureau of Economic Research, which circulates working papers) estimates about $100 billion of wealth tax ($104 billion in its Table 5, reproduced here as $103.8B), and $106.8B once the income tax the tax changes is added in. The JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026, revising the March 2026 version by Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso) finds that the tax would collect about $30 billion by its own account but leave the state $38.9 billion poorer in today's dollars (−$38.9B, a net present value), because billionaires who leave stop paying California income tax for ever. The three inputs that separate the two most: whether a one-time tax changes behaviour the way a permanent one would, how many billionaires had already left, and whether the lost income tax is counted for five years (BSZ) or for ever (JR). This page puts both on one common output and walks from one number to the other, one disputed input at a time. Each analysis is also a full Open Policy Analysis of its own; this page adds a comparison layer on top, following the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework.

OPA by Fernando Hoces de la Guardia. This site recommends no vote and favours neither analysis: it reproduces both analyses' arithmetic and shows where their assumptions differ.

BSZ main estimate1 $106.8B Net total: the wealth tax plus the income tax it changes, over 5 years, in today's dollars
JR main estimate1 −$38.9B Net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, in today's dollars, averaged over the authors' model runs (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026)

Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

1 Each analysis's own main estimate, defined its own way. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years: the wealth tax ($103.8B as reproduced; the paper prints $104B, Table 5 row 1), plus extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the present value of the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted for 5 years. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice, explained on the BSZ analysis overview. The JR analysis's is a perpetuity net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, averaged over the authors' Monte Carlo draws (the authors draw each uncertain input at random 100,000 times and average the result; Jaros and Rauh, NBER chapter c15504, Tax Policy and the Economy vol. 41, September 2026). The net fiscal effect, one shared definition explained under "A common output definition" below, puts both on the same footing, and the explorer walks from one to the other. On it the BSZ end is the same $106.8B, with the 5 years discounted over the JR analysis's 1.5% to 4.5% rate range instead of at 3% (a discount rate turns future dollars into today's dollars; both give $106.8B). The JR end is −$39.0B, the exact expectation of the same Monte Carlo, its average computed by formula; the authors' seeded run (one run of the random draws started from a fixed seed number, so it gives the same draws every time) gives −$38.9B.

A common output definition

one definition both numbers can be put on, and the conventions behind it

The two headlines answer different questions. The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman) reports how much the one-time tax collects. The JR analysis reports a net present value that subtracts income tax lost for ever. Before any step of a bridge means anything, both are put on the same two outputs. The explorer opens on (b), the net fiscal effect, because it is the question both main estimates answer; (a) is the secondary output, shown in the report.

In words: the net fiscal effect is the wealth tax collected, plus the extra income tax from people selling stock to pay it, minus the income tax lost to people who leave, counted for some number of years (the two analyses disagree on how many).

(b) net fiscal effect = (a) + extra income tax from assets sold to pay - present value of income tax lost to departures, over a horizon of H years (a) gross revenue = one-time wealth tax collected

The horizon H is itself disputed (row 2 of the project's list of 8 disputed inputs, comparison/DISPUTES.md). The BSZ analysis counts the losses for about the 5 payment years, the JR analysis for ever. It is therefore a slider in each panel of the explorer, not a fixed choice; the bridge uses each analysis's own horizon and shows the switch as one of its steps. Discounting uses the JR analysis's own range for the discount rate, r uniform between 1.5% and 4.5%. The BSZ analysis takes no position on r.

Conventions. All amounts are nominal billions of dollars. The wealth tax is counted at its face value when assessed (the BSZ analysis values the base on 1 July 2026; the JR analysis's September version grows the base it used in March, the 2025 Forbes list, by 7% to 31 December 2026), undiscounted although the Act collects it in five yearly installments; both analyses do this, so the comparison follows them. Present values are at the assessment date. The 1.5% to 4.5% range is what the JR code draws; its code labels the draw (r − g), the rate less the growth of the lost income tax, and its text r (the JR analysis's mismatch 3).

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses, and so this OPA, take the measure as written (full text in docs/initiative/): the 5% rate, the 1 January 2026 residency date, the 31 December 2026 valuation date and payment in five yearly installments, with the wealth tax counted at its face value, not discounted over the five years. Neither analysis estimates the day-to-day movement of the Forbes base (GGSS, the Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske expert report written in support of the measure, note a range of $87 billion to $107 billion between January and July 2026, p.4 fn.8). The BSZ analysis alone adds the income tax from assets sold to pay the tax; the JR analysis alone weights the revenue by the chance the Act survives in court. Nothing is added to either here.

The existing comparison: Hoopes (2026)

the one published comparison, read off its chart and set beside this bridge

Jeffrey Hoopes, "Galle v Rauh: Comparing Two Revenue Estimates for California Billionaire Taxation" (SSRN 6428578, 7 pages), is the one published comparison of the two estimates. It compares their earlier versions: the February 2026 text of the GGSS expert report (Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske, written in support of the measure), on which the BSZ analysis builds, and the March 2026 version of the JR analysis (Rauh et al., SSRN, a preprint site). His Figure 2 (p.6) is a waterfall from the "Galle estimate" to "Rauh net fiscal effect". It prints no values, so the numbers in its column below are read off the chart: bar edges measured in a 300 dpi raster against the y-axis ticks (tick fit within $0.1B; reading uncertainty about $0.6B per bar; script comparison/tools/digitize_hoopes_fig2.py). Beside them is our bridge read in the same order, starting at the same printed $100B and ending at the March 2026 version, before the September revision.

Disclosures from the note itself: Hoopes states his priors, "To lay my cards on the table, I believe that the world is complicated, and that when possible, empirical analysis should be done in as detailed a way as possible" (p.2), and thanks "David Gamage for encouraging me to read both of these analyses carefully and compare the two" (p.1); Gamage is a GGSS co-author.

Every difference

  • Vintage. Hoopes's estimate uses the February GGSS text: 213 billionaires, $2,182B of wealth, $109B gross, $99B after 10% avoidance, rounded up to $100B (p.3 fn.1). The July GGSS text covers 250 billionaires and $2,307B: $103.8B after avoidance, rounded down to $100B. The same printed headline hides a rounding of +$1B in one vintage and of −$3.8B in the other.
  • Residency. His bar is −$12.6B, close to Rauh's own −$12.84B for dropping Ellison, Houston and Snyder and adding Sacks (Rauh p.5). By July the BSZ analysis has itself dropped Ellison, so our step is a different quantity: the $150B of non-US-citizen residents Rauh omits plus the list and valuation-date difference, −$18.5B in one order.
  • Confirmed departures. His bar is −$17.0B; Rauh's own step is −$26.69B before avoidance (94.2 to 67.51, p.11), and ours is −$24.2B at that point of the order. We could not trace his figure to a number in Rauh et al.
  • Additional movers. His −$12.5B matches Rauh's expanded ten-departure estimate (67.51 to 55.1, a step of −$12.41B, pp.12-13). The March 2026 version's own −$24.7B mean NPV does not use that estimate, so our bridge has no such step.
  • Behaviour. His −$9.2B is close to Rauh's step from the expanded estimate to the literature calibration (55.1 to 45.59, −$9.51B, p.14). Ours, −$9.5B in one order, nets three labelled steps: dropping the 10.0% avoidance allowance (up), estimating the one-time tax as a permanent rate (down) and the elasticity from 10.00 to 12.58 (down).
  • Real estate. His bar is drawn near zero (−$1.1B ± 0.8 read off the chart), while his text says the exclusion reduces the base by about $8B (p.4). The two are consistent: $8B of base is about $0.4B of revenue at 5%. Ours is −$0.24B.
  • Level before income tax. His revenue level before the income-tax bar is about $47.2B; Rauh's preferred estimate is 'about $40B' (p.14) and the expected revenue in the Monte Carlo behind its mean NPV is $51.26B. Our bridge uses the last, $51.26B.
  • Income tax. His single bar, −$71.5B, ends at −$24.3B, close to the March 2026 version's −$24.7B. That version's own expected present value of lost income tax is −$75.96B against $51.26B of revenue, so with a different starting level his bar works as a plug. We split it into four labelled steps (proportionality, the income tax level, the horizon, asset-sale tax), which interact with the base steps.
  • Order and labels. His is one sequential order, with no provenance labels; ours shows Shapley averages over all orders (a Shapley value: a step's effect averaged over every order the inputs could be switched in, so the steps add up to the gap) as well as one order, with each step labelled data, research, guesswork or scenario.
  • Steps he does not have. The non-US-citizen residents, the BSZ analysis's avoidance allowance being dropped, the asset-sale income tax, and the September 2026 revision (which postdates his note).
