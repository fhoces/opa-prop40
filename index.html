Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Reconciliation of the Two Analyses

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax Californians worth $1 billion or more once, at 5% of their net worth, paid over five years. The BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman, NBER Working Paper 35218, August 2026) finds that it raises $106.8B net of the income tax it changes. The JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026) finds that it leaves the state $38.9B poorer, because billionaires who leave stop paying California income tax for ever. This page puts both on one definition and shows which assumptions make the difference. It follows the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework.

BSZ main estimate1 $106.8B Net total: the wealth tax plus the income tax it changes, over 5 years, in today's dollars
JR main estimate1 −$38.9B Net present value: the wealth tax minus the income tax lost for ever, in today's dollars, averaged over the authors' model runs (Jaros and Rauh, September 2026)

Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

What separates the two numbers

$106.8BBSZ estimate −$45.0BChance the tax survives litigation −$33.6BHow long the income tax loss lasts −$33.5BShare of billionaire income tax lost −$33.8Bthe 7 other inputs together ≈−$39.0BJR estimate

Start from the BSZ estimate and take away what each disagreement costs: each term is that input's effect on the net fiscal effect when switched from the BSZ value to the JR value, averaged over every order the inputs could be switched in (its Shapley value). The terms add up exactly. The JR estimate here is the exact mean of the authors' Monte Carlo, −$39.0B; their seeded run prints −$38.9B. The report gives every step.

A common output definition

one definition both numbers can be put on, and the conventions behind it

The two headlines answer different questions: the BSZ analysis reports what the tax collects, the JR analysis a net present value that subtracts income tax lost for ever. Both are put on two outputs. The explorer shows (b); (a) is in the report.

(b) net fiscal effect = (a) + extra income tax from assets sold to pay - present value of income tax lost to departures, over a horizon of H years (a) gross revenue = one-time wealth tax collected

The horizon H is itself disputed: about the 5 payment years for the BSZ analysis, for ever for the JR analysis. So it is a dial in the explorer and a step of the bridge. Present values use the JR analysis's discount-rate range, 1.5% to 4.5%; the BSZ analysis takes no position on the rate.

Conventions. Nominal billions of dollars. The wealth tax is counted at face value when assessed and not discounted over the five installments, as both analyses do. The BSZ analysis values the base on 1 July 2026; the JR analysis grows its 2025 Forbes base by 7% to 31 December 2026. The JR code labels its rate draw r − g and its text r (the JR analysis's mismatch 3).

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses, and so this OPA, take the measure as written (docs/initiative/). Neither models the day-to-day movement of the Forbes base. Nothing is added to either analysis here.

The existing comparison: Hoopes (2026)

the one published comparison, read off its chart and set beside this bridge

Jeffrey Hoopes, "Galle v Rauh: Comparing Two Revenue Estimates for California Billionaire Taxation" (SSRN 6428578), is the one published comparison. It compares earlier versions: the February 2026 GGSS expert report (Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske), on which the BSZ analysis builds, and the March 2026 version of the JR analysis (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso). His Figure 2 prints no values, so his column is read off the chart (tick fit within $0.1B, about $0.6B per bar; comparison/tools/digitize_hoopes_fig2.py). Beside it is our bridge in his order, ending at the September 2026 version.

Disclosures from the note itself: Hoopes states his priors, "To lay my cards on the table, I believe that the world is complicated, and that when possible, empirical analysis should be done in as detailed a way as possible" (p.2), and thanks "David Gamage for encouraging me to read both of these analyses carefully and compare the two" (p.1); Gamage is a GGSS co-author.

Every difference

  • Vintage. Hoopes's estimate uses the February GGSS text: 213 billionaires, $2,182B of wealth, $109B gross, $99B after 10% avoidance, rounded up to $100B (p.3 fn.1). The July GGSS text covers 250 billionaires and $2,307B: $103.8B after avoidance, rounded down to $100B. The same printed headline hides a rounding of +$1B in one vintage and of −$3.8B in the other.
  • Residency. His bar is −$12.6B, close to Rauh's own −$12.84B for dropping Ellison, Houston and Snyder and adding Sacks (Rauh p.5). By July the BSZ analysis has itself dropped Ellison, so our step is a different quantity: the $150B of non-US-citizen residents Rauh omits plus the list and valuation-date difference, −$12.6B in one order.
  • Confirmed departures. His bar is −$17.0B; Rauh's own step is −$26.69B before avoidance (94.2 to 67.51, p.11), and ours is −$26.0B at that point of the order. We could not trace his figure to a number in Rauh et al.
  • Additional movers. His −$12.5B matches Rauh's expanded ten-departure estimate (67.51 to 55.1, a step of −$12.41B, pp.12-13). The March 2026 version's own −$24.7B mean NPV does not use that estimate, so our bridge has no such step.
  • Behaviour. His −$9.2B is close to the March 2026 version's step from the expanded estimate to the literature calibration (55.1 to 45.59, −$9.51B, p.14), a mobility response from a European elasticity. The September 2026 version predicts no such departures; in their place it weights the revenue by the chance the tax survives litigation. Ours, −$29.0B in one order, nets two labelled steps: dropping the 10.0% avoidance allowance (up) and the litigation weight (down).
  • Real estate. His bar is drawn near zero (−$1.1B ± 0.8 read off the chart), while his text says the exclusion reduces the base by about $8B (p.4). The two are consistent: $8B of base is about $0.4B of revenue at 5%. Ours is −$0.23B.
  • Level before income tax. His revenue level before the income-tax bar is about $47.2B, near the March 2026 version's preferred 'about $40B' (p.14). Ours ends at $36.00B, the expected revenue in the Monte Carlo behind the September 2026 version's mean NPV (its text prefers 'approximately $30B', p.1).
  • Income tax. His single bar, −$71.5B, ends at −$24.3B, close to the March 2026 version's −$24.7B. Ours is −$74.98B, the September 2026 version's expected present value of lost income tax against $36.00B of revenue, and we split it into four labelled steps (the share lost, the income tax level, the horizon, asset-sale tax), which interact with the base steps.
  • Order and labels. His is one sequential order, with no provenance labels; ours shows Shapley averages over all orders (a Shapley value: a step's effect averaged over every order the inputs could be switched in, so the steps add up to the gap) as well as one order, with each step labelled data, research, guesswork or scenario.
  • Steps he does not have. The non-US-citizen residents, the BSZ analysis's avoidance allowance being dropped, the asset-sale income tax, and the litigation weight of the September 2026 version (which postdates his note).

1 Each analysis's own main estimate. The BSZ analysis's is a net total over 5 years at 3%: the wealth tax ($103.8B; the paper prints $104B, Table 5 row 1), plus the income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the income tax lost to those who leave. BSZ's own headline is the wealth tax alone; the net total is this OPA's choice (BSZ analysis overview).
