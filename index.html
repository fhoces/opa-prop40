Open Policy Analysis: reconciliation of two rival analyses

California's Billionaire Tax (Proposition 40): why the two revenue estimates differ

Supporters score the one-time 5% tax on the net worth of California's billionaires at about $100B; the RJKDC report (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso) finds −$24.7B once lost income tax is netted out. This page puts both on one common output and walks from one number to the other, one disputed input at a time. Each side is also a full, independent Open Policy Analysis of its own; this page adds a reconciliation layer on top, following the Open Policy Analysis framework.

A common output definition

The two headlines answer different questions. The BSZ side (Boll, Saez and Zucman, plus the GGSS expert report by Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske) reports how much the one-time tax collects; the RJKDC side reports a net present value that subtracts income tax lost for ever. Before any step of a bridge means anything, both are put on the same two outputs:

(a) gross revenue = one-time wealth tax collected (b) net fiscal effect = (a) + extra income tax from assets sold to pay - present value of income tax lost to departures, over a horizon of H years

The horizon H is itself disputed (row 2 of the project's list of 8 disputed inputs, comparison/DISPUTES.md): the BSZ side counts the losses for about the 5 payment years, the RJKDC side for ever. It is therefore a dial on the bridge below, not a fixed choice. Discounting uses the RJKDC report's own range for the discount rate, r uniform between 1.5% and 4.5% (Rauh p.20 eq.24); the BSZ side takes no position on r.

The bridge, one disputed input at a time

One scoring function, score_common() (comparison/R/model.R, with a Python twin), scores both sides. Every disputed input is an argument that takes either the BSZ value or Rauh's. With every input at the BSZ value it returns BSZ Table 5 row 1 exactly ($103.815B); with every input at Rauh's value it returns the expectation of Rauh's own Monte Carlo up to a $0.10B model difference that is shown as its own step. The bridge runs in the direction of Figure 2 of Hoopes (2026), the existing comparison described below, from GGSS to Rauh; BSZ row 1 is the checkpoint. Because the inputs interact (a departure matters more when the base is larger), the size of a step depends on the order the inputs are switched. The default bars are Shapley values: each step's switch averaged over all 39,916,800 possible orders, so the steps add up exactly to the total gap whatever the reading order. "One order" shows a single sequence instead, the one Hoopes reads in.

What the Shapley and one-order steps say

  • Output (a) falls from $103.8B to $51.3B. The three largest Shapley steps are billionaires already departed (−$21.3B, guesswork on data); one-time tax scored as a permanent 5 pp rate (−$21.1B, scenario + guesswork); base list and valuation date (−$9.4B, data).
  • One step favours the BSZ side's number going up: Rauh applies no avoidance or evasion allowance beyond migration (his semi-elasticity is the migration share of Brulhart et al.'s response, Rauh pp.13-14), so dropping GGSS's 10% adds +$8.0B (Shapley). DISPUTES.md does not list this as a row.
  • Order matters most for scoring the one-time tax as a permanent 5-percentage-point rate: −$21.1B averaged over all orders, −$10.2B in the one order shown. Rauh's revenue is uniform between a confirmed-departures ceiling and an elasticity floor, so the elasticity bites only on top of whatever departures are already in: the step is large when switched early and smaller when switched after the departures.
  • For output (b) the horizon is the single largest step read in Hoopes's order (−$66.5B, the last switch, when the loss is already Rauh-sized) but only −$27.1B averaged over orders, because a perpetuity multiplies whatever annual loss the other inputs imply. The annual loss runs from $0.15B (BSZ) to $2.07B (Rauh).
  • Model differences are small and shown, not hidden: −$0.0003B on (a) and +$0.10B on (b), from Rauh's rounded literals (MISMATCHES #6 in rjkdc-analysis). Rauh's printed −$24.7B is the expectation of his Monte Carlo (−$24.707B); his seed-2026 run gives −$24.77B.
  • The NBER revision is one further step: −$15.3B on (a) and −$14.3B on (b). Its printed −$38.9B is one Monte Carlo run; the expectation is −$38.98B.

The existing comparison: Hoopes (2026)

Jeffrey Hoopes, "Galle v Rauh: Comparing Two Revenue Estimates for California Billionaire Taxation" (SSRN 6428578, 7 pages), is the one published side-by-side of the two estimates. His Figure 2 (p.6) is a waterfall from the "Galle estimate" to "Rauh net fiscal effect". It prints no values, so the numbers in its column below are read off the chart: bar edges measured in a 300 dpi raster against the y-axis ticks (tick fit within $0.1B; reading uncertainty about $0.6B per bar; script comparison/tools/digitize_hoopes_fig2.py). Beside them is our bridge read in the same order, starting at the same printed $100B.

Disclosures from the note itself: Hoopes states his priors, "To lay my cards on the table, I believe that the world is complicated, and that when possible, empirical analysis should be done in as detailed a way as possible" (p.2), and thanks "David Gamage for encouraging me to read both of these analyses carefully and compare the two" (p.1); Gamage is a GGSS co-author.

Every difference

  • Vintage. Hoopes scores the February GGSS text: 213 billionaires, $2,182B of wealth, $109B gross, $99B after 10% avoidance, rounded up to $100B (p.3 fn.1). The July GGSS text scores 250 billionaires and $2,307B: $103.8B after avoidance, rounded down to $100B. The same printed headline hides a rounding of +$1B in one vintage and of −$3.8B in the other.
  • Residency. His bar is −$12.6B, close to Rauh's own −$12.84B for dropping Ellison, Houston and Snyder and adding Sacks (Rauh p.5). By July the BSZ side has itself dropped Ellison, so our step is a different quantity: the $150B of non-US-citizen residents Rauh omits plus the list and valuation-date difference, −$18.5B in one order.
  • Confirmed departures. His bar is −$17.0B; Rauh's own step is −$26.69B before avoidance (94.2 to 67.51, p.11), and ours is −$24.2B at that point of the order. We could not trace his figure to a number in Rauh et al.
  • Additional movers. His −$12.5B matches Rauh's expanded ten-departure estimate (67.51 to 55.1, a step of −$12.41B, pp.12-13). Rauh's own −$24.7B headline does not use that estimate, so our bridge has no such step.
  • Behaviour. His −$9.2B is close to Rauh's step from the expanded estimate to the literature calibration (55.1 to 45.59, −$9.51B, p.14). Ours, −$9.5B in one order, nets three labelled steps: dropping the 10.0% avoidance allowance (up), scoring the one-time tax as a permanent rate (down) and the elasticity from 10.00 to 12.58 (down).
  • Real estate. His bar is drawn near zero (−$1.1B ± 0.8 read off the chart), while his text says the exclusion reduces the base by about $8B (p.4). The two are consistent: $8B of base is about $0.4B of revenue at 5%. Ours is −$0.24B.
  • Level before income tax. His revenue level before the income-tax bar is about $47.2B; Rauh's preferred estimate is 'about $40B' (p.14) and the expected revenue in the Monte Carlo behind the headline is $51.26B. Our bridge uses the last, $51.26B.
  • Income tax. His single bar, −$71.5B, ends at −$24.3B, close to Rauh's −$24.7B. Rauh's own expected present value of lost income tax is −$75.96B against $51.26B of revenue, so with a different starting level his bar works as a plug. We split it into four labelled steps (proportionality, the income tax level, the horizon, asset-sale tax), which interact with the base steps.
  • Order and labels. His is one sequential order, with no provenance labels; ours shows Shapley averages over all orders as well as one order, with each step labelled data, research, guesswork or scenario.
  • Steps he does not have. The non-US-citizen residents, the BSZ side's avoidance allowance being dropped, the asset-sale income tax, and the NBER revision (which postdates his note).

Neutral material and credited alternative dials

LAO (neutral context). The Legislative Analyst's ballot analysis gives no point estimate: the tax "probably would collect tens of billions of dollars", and responses "probably would reduce state revenues by less than $1 billion per year" (LAO p.2). It enters here only as context. Walczak / California Tax Foundation (22 Apr 2026) is same-side work on the RJKDC side, credited as an alternative dial on the annual loss, never merged into Rauh's headline: his $3.53-4.49B per year includes spillovers to sales and other taxes (p.1).
