How much would the one-time billionaire tax raise?

The BSZ side scores Proposition 40 at about $100 billion. That number is simple arithmetic on the Forbes list of California billionaires plus a handful of assumptions, and most of those assumptions are guesswork: nobody has measured them. The inputs below are the five guesses among them, plus one research-based switch (counting the billionaires Forbes misses, from a Pareto extrapolation). The two switches, that one and the aggressive assumption on who has left, reproduce rows 2 to 4 of Table 5 of Boll, Saez and Zucman (BSZ, 2026).

This page is the open output of the BSZ side. Every combination of the inputs is precomputed by the same R pipeline that reproduces the authors' workbook, so the numbers update as you click.

Where this tool departs from the workbook. Two readings the workbook leaves implicit, both kept at the workbook's own behaviour.

(1) The income tax lost to leavers is typed as a literal 5% in Tab5!H6. The row label says "10% evasion/avoidance (half due to mobility)", so the explorer reads that 5% as avoidance times the share that is leaving. At the authors' values the two are identical; elsewhere this is our reading.

(2) Table 5's row 2 subtracts a deduction for billionaires in the $1B to $1.1B phase-in band, but row 4, which adds the same billionaires, does not (Tab5!F9). The explorer copies this, so whenever both switches are on, revenue is higher than a consistent treatment would give.

The income tax loss is per year. How many years it lasts is not part of Table 5: the authors argue about 5 (BSZ PDF p.27), the RJKDC side (Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso, who oppose the measure) treats it as permanent. That horizon is the main dispute between the two sides; it belongs to the project's reconciliation page, not to this side's output.
