Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Analysis From Boll, Saez, Zucman (BSZ)

This OPA site: AI generated, partially reviewed

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax people who lived in California on 1 January 2026 and are worth $1 billion or more, once, at 5% of their net worth. Boll, Saez and Zucman (BSZ) estimate it raises about $100 billion. This Open Policy Analysis (OPA) reproduces that estimate, adds its income tax effects to get the main estimate below, and marks which inputs are data and which are guesses.

Main estimate1, base scenario (the authors' main case, row 1 of their Table 5) $106.8B Defined as the wealth tax, plus the extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted over 5 years (present value at 3% a year). The wealth tax is counted at face value. More in the report.

1 BSZ's headline is the wealth tax alone: $104 billion in their Table 5, $103.8 billion here. This OPA adds the income tax effects to compare with the JR analysis. Why.

Difference between this OPA and the original analysis #

The claims are the authors'. What this OPA changes is what a reader can check and change.

Everything of substance on this site belongs to the original analysis: Boll, Saez and Zucman, California Billionaires: Wealth, Taxes, and Wealth Tax Revenue Estimates, NBER Working Paper 35218 (August 2026). The data work, the method, the four scenarios of Table 5 and every conclusion drawn from them are the authors' work. This site adds no data and no new model.

The authors shared a replication bundle of code and data with this project, on condition that it not be shared publicly. The numbers on this page come from the public workbook.

This OPA rebuilds Table 5 and most of the paper's tables and figures in R, with a Python twin and a test suite. It sorts every input by origin: data, research or guesswork. Its main estimate nets the income tax effects into one total, which is this OPA's choice, not the authors' (footnote 1). The explorer adds settings the authors did not publish. Where this site and the paper disagree, both numbers are shown, and the paper is the reference.

What the model says #

This OPA's main estimate is the base scenario of Table 5 in Boll, Saez and Zucman (2026), net of its income tax effects. The wealth tax is 5% of the $2,307 billion that Forbes lists for 250 California billionaires on 1 July 2026, less 10% for avoidance and evasion: $103.8 billion, collected once (the paper prints $104 billion; the abstract says about $100 billion). Selling assets to pay it adds $3.68B of income tax. Leavers take $0.15B a year with them, worth $0.70B over 5 years at 3%. The total is $106.8 billion.1 The other rows are the authors' own sensitivity checks.

The yearly income tax loss has no end date in Table 5. The authors argue the losses should be temporary and give five years, the payment period, as an example ("If they last say only 5 years", PDF p.27). The main estimate's five years are this OPA's reading of that example. The JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh; first version Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso) counts it forever; BSZ call treating the responses as permanent "the main flaw in the scoring analysis of Rauh et al." (PDF p.22, fn.29). The JR analysis assumes leavers do not come back, because the Act permanently lifts the constitutional cap on taxes on intangible property and lets the Legislature change the Act by a two-thirds vote (chapter PDF p.32). The revenue itself moves with only two guesses: the avoidance rate, and whether anyone left California before 1 January 2026. See every input, by origin.

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses take the measure as written (docs/initiative/), with the wealth tax at face value. Neither counts the deferral charge of 7.5% a year that the Act adds to installment payments. Neither predicts the Forbes base on the 31 December 2026 valuation date. The BSZ analysis uses the 1 July 2026 list (BSZ's website tracks the estimate day by day, PDF p.23), and the JR analysis grows the 2025 list by 7%. The BSZ analysis alone counts the income tax from asset sales and the non-US-citizen residents. The JR analysis alone weights the revenue by the chance the Act survives in court.
