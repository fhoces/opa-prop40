Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Analysis From Boll, Saez, Zucman (BSZ)

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax people who lived in California on 1 January 2026 and are worth $1 billion or more: once, at 5% of their whole net worth as valued on 31 December 2026, paid in five equal yearly installments. Boll, Saez and Zucman (BSZ) estimate it at about $100 billion ($104 billion in their Table 5; NBER Working Paper 35218, August 2026; NBER is the National Bureau of Economic Research, which circulates working papers). This site reproduces that estimate from the paper and its workbook, and marks which of its inputs are data and which are guesses. This site follows the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework, so what the analysis concludes, how it gets there, and what to run yourself stay in separate, checkable places.

OPA by Fernando Hoces de la Guardia. This site recommends no vote and favours neither analysis: it reproduces both analyses' arithmetic and shows where their assumptions differ.

Main estimate1, base scenario (the authors' main case, row 1 of their Table 5) $106.8B Defined as the wealth tax, plus the extra income tax from assets sold to pay it, minus the income tax lost to billionaires who leave, counted over 5 years in today's dollars (present value at 3% a year). The authors' own headline is the wealth tax alone; this total is this OPA's choice (footnote 1).

1 The main estimate is not the authors' choice: BSZ's headline is the wealth tax alone, about $100 billion in the abstract and $104 billion in Table 5, reproduced here as $103.8 billion. What the model says

This OPA's main estimate is the base scenario of Table 5 in Boll, Saez and Zucman (2026), net of its income tax effects. The wealth tax is 5% of the $2,307 billion that Forbes lists for 250 California billionaires on 1 July 2026, less 10% for avoidance and evasion: $103.8 billion, collected once (the paper prints $104 billion; the abstract says about $100 billion). Selling assets to pay it adds $3.68B of income tax. Leavers take $0.15B a year with them, worth $0.70B over 5 years at 3%. The total is $106.8 billion.1 The other rows are the authors' own sensitivity checks.

The yearly income tax loss has no end date in Table 5. The authors argue it should last about as long as the five payment years; the JR analysis (Jaros and Rauh; first version Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso) counts it forever; BSZ call treating the responses as permanent "the main flaw in the scoring analysis of Rauh et al." (PDF p.22, fn.29). The revenue itself moves with only two guesses: the avoidance rate, and whether anyone left California before 1 January 2026. See every input, by origin.

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses, and so this OPA, take the measure as written (full text in docs/initiative/): the 5% rate, the 1 January 2026 residency date, the 31 December 2026 valuation date and payment in five yearly installments, with the wealth tax counted at its face value, not discounted over the five years. Neither analysis estimates the day-to-day movement of the Forbes base (GGSS, the Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske expert report written in support of the measure, note a range of $87 billion to $107 billion between January and July 2026, p.4 fn.8). The BSZ analysis alone adds the income tax from assets sold to pay the tax; the JR analysis alone weights the revenue by the chance the Act survives in court. Nothing is added to either here.
