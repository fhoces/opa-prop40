What the Billionaire Tax Act is worth to the state, by the JR method

Jaros and Rauh (JR, NBER, September 2026) find that the one-time billionaire tax leaves California poorer on average. The wealth tax is collected once, but billionaires who leave stop paying income tax for ever. Four of the authors' five inputs below are guesswork; the extra litigation weight and further growth dials are this OPA's additions. Move them and the estimate updates.

NPV = WT − f·C / (r − g). The wealth tax collected once (WT), minus the income tax lost for ever when billionaires leave: the share that leaves (f) times the yearly tax (C), divided by the discount rate net of the tax's growth (r − g). Averaged over 100,000 random draws.

Each setting shows the exact average of the authors' formula, not a new random run. At the main setting that is The printed figure comes from one seeded run, and the gap is sampling error (report, Step 6). The input labels follow the report's input table. The litigation dial reads the chapter's q (PDF pp.19-20), which the code does not apply separately (mismatch 5). The comparison explorer's litigation dial stands at one half at the JR end. That is this same half, which here comes from the revenue draw's 0 floor. The growth dial is this OPA's illustration of growth beyond the authors' r − g (mismatch 3).

The share below zero sums up the authors' independent, uniform draws over ranges that are themselves guesswork. It is not a probability that the tax loses money: it moves with those ranges, as the explorer's Rate 1.5% and Rate 4.5% settings show.
