Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Analysis From Jaros and Rauh (JR) Work in progress

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax people who lived in California on 1 January 2026 and are worth $1 billion or more: once, at 5% of their whole net worth as valued on 31 December 2026, paid in five equal yearly installments. The RJKDC analysis argues the state ends up poorer, because billionaires who leave take their income tax with them for ever. This site reproduces that estimate from Jaros and Rauh, The Net Present Value of the California Billionaire Tax Act (NBER chapter c15504, 22 September 2026; NBER is the National Bureau of Economic Research, which circulates working papers), the revision of the first version by Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso (SSRN 6340778, March 2026). This site calls the analysis RJKDC, after the five authors of that first version; the title names the two authors of the version reproduced. It is rebuilt from the authors' own public code and data (github.com/bjaros20/wealth_tax), and it marks which of its inputs are data and which are guesses. This site follows the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework, so what the analysis concludes, how it gets there, and what to run yourself stay in separate, checkable places.

OPA by Fernando Hoces de la Guardia. This site recommends no vote and favours neither analysis: it reproduces both analyses' arithmetic and shows where their assumptions differ.

What the model says

The Act collects a wealth tax once; billionaires who leave stop paying California income tax for ever. The authors value that lost stream as a perpetuity (the same amount every year for ever, worth 1/r times the yearly amount today at discount rate r) and subtract it: NPV = revenue − (share of the income tax base that leaves) × (annual income tax) / (discount rate). They report the average NPV over 100,000 random draws of those inputs: the main estimate, −$38.9 billion (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026).1 Amounts are billions of dollars.

1 The authors' own headline (NBER PDF p.1). The comparison overview sets it beside the BSZ analysis.

Printed values from NBER PDF pp.1, 23 and 35. Three things the numbers show. First, the result is driven by the loss term: roughly 75 of present value lost against a few tens collected, because at the draws' 1.5% to 4.5% rates a dollar a year for ever is worth 22 to 67 dollars today. Second, the paper says the tax would collect about $30 billion (p.23: half of the base it recognizes after the pre-filing and Zuckerberg exclusions). The simulation behind the headline uses more: it draws revenue anywhere between 0 and 72, the most the tax could collect after the seven confirmed departures, with a mean of 36, the paper's litigation-discounted ceiling (0.5 × 72, its central revenue, p.33). So the headline rests on a revenue above the one the paper prints as collectible. Third, of the seven inputs that move the answer, six are guesswork; the explorer shows how far each one moves it. Whether the income tax loss really lasts for ever is the main point the BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman) disputes.

What this OPA does not estimate. Both analyses, and so this OPA, take the measure as written (full text in docs/initiative/): the 5% rate, the 1 January 2026 residency date, the 31 December 2026 valuation date and payment in five yearly installments, with the wealth tax counted at its face value, not discounted over the five years. Neither analysis estimates the day-to-day movement of the Forbes base (GGSS, the Galle, Gamage, Saez and Shanske expert report written in support of the measure, note a range of $87 billion to $107 billion between January and July 2026, p.4 fn.8). The BSZ analysis alone adds the income tax from assets sold to pay the tax; the RJKDC analysis alone weights the revenue by the chance the Act survives in court. Nothing is added to either here.

Along the way we found nine mismatches between the authors' text, their READMEs and their code. Four of them concern this analysis.3 We reproduce the code as shipped and record each one:

  1. The code and eq. 18 call the 1.5% to 4.5% draw (r − g): r is the real discount rate and g the growth rate of the lost income tax. The sentence after eq. 19 divides by r alone (p.35), so one interval stands for two concepts.
  2. The revenue ceiling is 72 in the script, 72.06 in the README and in the paper's eq. 4 (p.22), and 72.05 in the person-level data.
  3. The litigation weight q = 0.50, the chance the Act survives in court: the code never multiplies by q; drawing revenue uniformly from 0 to 72 gives the same mean, 0.5 × 72, but a different distribution.
  4. The baseline revenue sums to 94.30 in the authors' workbook, and the grown person-level data give the same; the text prints 94.20 (p.19).
