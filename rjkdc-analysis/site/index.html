Open Policy Analysis for:

California Proposition 40, Analysis From Jaros and Rauh (JR) AI generated, partially reviewed

Proposition 40, the Billionaire Tax Act, would tax Californians worth $1 billion or more once, at 5% of their net worth, paid over five years. The JR analysis argues the state ends up poorer, because billionaires who leave take their income tax with them for ever. This site reproduces Jaros and Rauh, The Net Present Value of the California Billionaire Tax Act (NBER, September 2026),1 from the authors' own code and data. It marks which inputs are data and which are guesses. It follows the Open Policy Analysis (OPA) framework, so the result, the method and the materials each have their own page.

Main estimate1, NBER September 2026 −$38.9B Defined as the one-time wealth tax revenue minus the present value of California income tax lost for ever to billionaires who leave, averaged over 100,000 random runs of the authors' model (a Monte Carlo simulation). Today's dollars (present value): what money received or lost in future years is worth now.

The loss decides the sign. The income tax lost for ever is worth about $75B today, against $36B of wealth tax collected once (what the model says).

1 Jaros and Rauh, NBER chapter c15504, Tax Policy and the Economy vol. 41, PDF p.1. The figure is the average of one seeded run of the authors' simulation (a fixed starting number, so the same draws every time). It revises a March 2026 first version by Rauh, Jaros, Kearney, Doran and Cosso (footnote 2).

Difference between this OPA and the original analysis #

Everything of substance on this site belongs to the original analysis: Jaros and Rauh, The Net Present Value of the California Billionaire Tax Act, NBER chapter c15504 (Tax Policy and the Economy vol. 41, September 2026). The person-by-person base, the observed departures, the litigation weight, the perpetuity, the Monte Carlo and every conclusion drawn from them are the authors' work. This site adds no data and no new model.

The original analysis publishes all three layers. The chapter gives the output (a mean net present value of −$38.9 billion, p.1) and the details (its equations and their sources). The authors' public repository, github.com/bjaros20/wealth_tax (MIT licence), gives the materials: workbooks, R scripts and person-level data. What a reader cannot easily see is where the three disagree. The text, the READMEs and the code differ in nine places across the two versions, four of them in the September version (see the materials page).

So the difference is not in what is claimed but in what can be checked. This OPA rebuilds the analysis in R, with a Python twin and a test suite. It runs the authors' code as shipped and records each mismatch rather than fixing it. Beside the printed −$38.9 billion from one seeded run, it computes the exact expectation of the same draws, −$38.98 billion, a number that does not depend on the seed. It labels each input as data or guesswork. Where this site and the chapter disagree, both numbers are shown, and the chapter is the reference.

What the model says #

The Act collects a wealth tax once; billionaires who leave stop paying California income tax for ever. The authors value that lost stream as a perpetuity (the same amount every year for ever, worth 1/r times the yearly amount today at discount rate r) and subtract it: NPV = revenue − (share of the income tax base that leaves) × (annual income tax) / (discount rate). They report the average NPV over 100,000 random draws of those inputs: the main estimate, −$38.9 billion (Jaros and Rauh, NBER, September 2026).1 Amounts are billions of dollars.

Printed values are from NBER PDF pp.1, 23 and 35. The numbers show three things. First, the result is driven by the loss term: roughly 75 of present value lost against 36 collected. At the draws' 1.5% to 4.5% rates, a dollar a year for ever is worth 22 to 67 dollars today.

Second, the paper says the tax would collect about $30 billion (p.23). The simulation behind the headline draws revenue anywhere from 0 to 72, the most left after the seven publicly reported moves. That draw averages 36 (0.5 × 72, p.33), above the 30 the paper calls collectible.

Third, of the seven inputs that move the answer, six are guesswork. The explorer shows how far each one moves it. Whether the income tax loss really lasts for ever is the main point the BSZ analysis (Boll, Saez and Zucman) disputes.

What this OPA does not estimate. It takes the measure as written (docs/initiative/), with the wealth tax at face value. The BSZ analysis alone adds the income tax from assets sold to pay the tax. The JR analysis alone weights the revenue by the chance the Act survives in court. Nothing is added to either here.

"Match" means the reproduced value rounds to the printed digit for deterministic figures, and lies within four Monte Carlo standard errors for simulated ones. The test suite holds every comparison; the materials page gives its current count.2

Along the way we found nine mismatches between the authors' text, their READMEs and their code. Four concern the September chapter. The other five concern only the March first version.3 We reproduce the code as shipped and record each one:

  1. The code and eq. 18 call the 1.5% to 4.5% draw (r − g): r is the real discount rate and g the growth rate of the lost income tax. The sentence after eq. 19 divides by r alone (p.35), so one interval stands for two concepts.
  2. The revenue ceiling is 72 in the script, 72.06 in the README and in the paper's eq. 4 (p.22), and 72.05 in the person-level data.
  3. The paper sets the litigation weight q, the chance the Act survives in court, at 0.50. The code never multiplies by q. Its revenue draw from 0 to 72 has the same mean, 0.5 × 72, but a different spread.
  4. The baseline revenue sums to 94.30 in the authors' workbook, and the grown person-level data give the same; the text prints 94.20 (p.19).
